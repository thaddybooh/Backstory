<Overview>

For most of October, Singapore has been living under a blanket of smoke. On 16 October the 24-hour Pollutant Standards Index (PSI) reached 229, deep in the “very unhealthy” range and the highest reading of the year, after twelve straight days of unhealthy air, the longest run since the great haze of 2015. The government handed out N95 masks from vending machines, kept schools running with windows shut and switched on medical subsidies for haze-related illness for the first time in eleven years.

The smoke comes from fires across Sumatra and Kalimantan (Indonesian Borneo), Indonesia’s worst fire season in more than a decade. Much of what is burning is peatland that has been drained to grow oil palm and paper trees, and an unusually strong El Niño has dried the region out. Malaysia has been hit as hard: schools across Johor were shut and an emergency was declared in part of Sarawak. In Indonesia itself, respiratory infections have soared and students are protesting against their own government.

Singapore is walking a careful line. It has offered Indonesia firefighting help and praised President Prabowo Subianto’s crackdown, while using its own haze law, its stock exchange and its banks to put pressure on the companies it says are linked to the fires. This issue explains why the region keeps burning, why Indonesia’s government has failed to stop it, and what Singapore can and cannot do about a problem that starts in another country.

</Overview>

## How to read the air

Haze coverage throws around two numbers, and it helps to know the difference.

The **PSI** (Pollutant Standards Index) is Singapore’s headline air-quality measure. It combines six pollutants, but during haze the one that matters is **PM2.5**: particles smaller than 2.5 micrometres, about thirty times thinner than a human hair. They are small enough to pass deep into the lungs and into the bloodstream, which is why haze is linked to asthma attacks, heart problems and, over the long run, early deaths. Singapore reports the PSI as a 24-hour average, useful for planning the next day, and PM2.5 as a 1-hour reading, useful for deciding whether to go for a run right now.

## Why the ground itself burns

To understand the haze you need to understand <mark>peat</mark>. In the coastal lowlands of Sumatra and Borneo, the ground in many places is not soil. It is a thick, spongy layer of partly rotted leaves, roots and wood, built up over thousands of years in swamps where the land was too waterlogged for dead plants to decompose. Some peat domes are several metres deep. Indonesia has some of the largest areas of tropical peat in the world.

<Sidenote term="Why peat smoke is so bad">A forest fire burns hot and fast in the open. A peat fire smoulders slowly underground at low temperature, which is incomplete burning. That produces far more smoke and fine particles per hectare, and the fire can keep going for weeks beneath the surface even after flames above are put out.</Sidenote>

Wet peat does not burn. The problem starts when people drain it. To plant oil palm or acacia (a fast-growing tree used for paper pulp), companies and farmers dig canals to lower the water table, because those crops can’t grow in a swamp. Drained peat dries out, shrinks and becomes, in effect, a giant underground pile of fuel. A spark from land clearing, a cigarette or a fire that escapes from a neighbouring plot can then set it alight, and the fire can creep underground and resurface far away.

Two details make the problem self-reinforcing. First, burned peat is easier to burn again: in this year’s data, about 40% of smoke sources were on land that had already burned at some point between 2015 and 2023. Second, burned land is rarely restored. Analyses of past fires found most burned peatland was later converted to plantations or left as scrub, which keeps it dry. Peat fires also release huge amounts of carbon, so the haze is a climate story as well as a health one.

## Who lights the fires, and why

Fire is the cheapest way to clear land in the tropics. Clearing by machine costs money; burning costs almost nothing, and the ash briefly fertilises the soil. That simple fact sits at the root of the haze, and it is why fires return every dry season even though burning is illegal for companies.

Indonesia is the world’s largest producer of palm oil, used in everything from instant noodles to shampoo to biodiesel, and a big producer of paper pulp. Both industries have expanded deep into Sumatra and Kalimantan over four decades, much of it onto peat. Who actually lights the fires is hotly contested.

**The case against the companies:** the Indonesian group Walhi found 74% of Kalimantan hotspots this year inside corporate concessions such as oil palm estates, mining permits and timber licences. A Greenpeace and Pantau Gambut report traced thousands of smoke sources to concessions and named groups linked to Malaysia and Singapore. Singapore’s own investigation linked three of the companies it served notices on to Asia Pulp & Paper, one of the world’s largest paper makers.

**The case that it is more complicated:** companies say fires often start on smallholder or disputed land and spread in, and a fire inside a concession does not prove the company lit it (Greenpeace’s own report says as much). Small farmers do burn; until this year Indonesian law let them burn up to two hectares under traditional practice. Land conflicts and illegal logging play a part too.

<Photo file="Kalimantan deforestation and degradation 4 (10706228444).jpg" alt="A burnt-out tree standing on cleared land in Central Kalimantan" caption="A burnt-out tree on cleared land in Central Kalimantan, where forest once stood." />

Either way, the haze is not a natural disaster. Drought makes it worse, but it starts with people draining peat and burning land because it is cheap.

## Why can’t Indonesia stop it?

This is the question many Indonesians are now asking. On 20 October, the second anniversary of Prabowo’s government, students protested in six cities; one banner read “If we burn, you burn with us!” By mid-October, acute respiratory infections in seven affected provinces had passed 283,000 cases, double the number a month earlier. It is tempting to blame one thing: greedy companies, corrupt officials or an incompetent president. The more accurate answer is that several problems are stacked on top of each other.

<WhyList>
<Why title="The state wants more plantations, not fewer">

Palm oil is a major export and supports millions of smallholders and workers in poor rural provinces, so any government that squeezes it risks rural livelihoods. Prabowo is pushing demand *up*. In July 2026 he rolled out “B50”, requiring diesel to be half palm-based biodiesel, to cut diesel imports made costly by the Iran war. He has also made food self-sufficiency a signature goal, clearing huge “food estates” in Papua. When the state wants farming to expand onto the same kinds of land that burn, fire prevention competes with its own priorities.

</Why>
<Why title="Land permits are tied up with local politics">

After the fall of the Suharto dictatorship in 1998, provinces and districts gained the power to issue plantation and mining permits. Local elections are expensive, and investigations by groups such as the Gecko Project have documented permits being used to fund campaigns or reward supporters. Riau, one of the worst fire provinces, has had governors jailed over forestry-permit corruption. Concession maps are kept secret and often overlap, so it is easy for everyone to deny responsibility for a burning patch of land.

</Why>
<Why title="Punishment rarely lands">

On paper the law is strict, and the government has won big court cases: courts have ordered companies to pay <mark>more than Rp 21 trillion (about US$1.3 billion) over fires, yet almost none has been collected</mark>. Companies declare bankruptcy, hide assets or keep replanting land they were ordered to restore. Permits are suspended rather than revoked, and arrests mostly catch small farmers rather than owners. A 2020 “Omnibus” jobs law also weakened strict liability for environmental damage.

</Why>
<Why title="The government cut its own firefighting capacity">

To fund flagship programmes such as free school meals, Prabowo’s government squeezed spending elsewhere. The national disaster agency’s budget fell from Rp 4.9 trillion in 2024 to a draft of Rp 491 billion for 2026, a cut of about 90%. The peatland restoration agency created after the 2015 disaster was dissolved in 2025. This month, investigators also charged five people with embezzling disaster relief funds, including the brother of the agency’s head, who was then removed.

</Why>
<Why title="The crisis was played down">

Researchers warned of a strong El Niño months ago. When the fires came, the government refused to declare a national disaster, even though Prabowo demanded exactly that as an opposition leader in 2015. He cited 639 hectares being fought as progress, while monitors estimate more than 1.9 million hectares burned this year. Malaysia’s offer of aircraft, made in September, went unanswered until mid-October.

</Why>
</WhyList>

### What about Prabowo himself?

Prabowo, a former general and onetime son-in-law of Suharto, comes from Indonesia’s business and political elite. A Tempo and Auriga Nusantara analysis counted about 362,000 hectares of business-use land linked to him. There is no evidence these holdings are among this year’s fires; critics’ point is broader, that the people making land policy often have a personal stake in land. His government has also shown it can act against companies when it wants to: in 2025 it seized about a million hectares of illegal plantations in forest zones, but handed most of it to a new state palm oil company rather than restoring it as forest. Since the haze began he has tightened the burning ban, ended the smallholder exemption, accepted foreign help and promised a new peatland agency. Whether that outlasts the rain, as past promises have not, is the real test.

<Answer title="So is it corruption, incompetence or economics?">

All three, in layers. **Economics** creates the incentive, and the state itself wants palm oil to grow. **Politics and corruption** protect the people who profit. **Weak enforcement** means even court victories don’t become penalties. And this year, **government choices** made it worse by cutting firefighting capacity and downplaying the crisis. Indonesia’s Supreme Court has already found the state negligent once, over the 2015 fires, and a new lawsuit against Prabowo’s government was filed in September.

</Answer>

## Why this year is so bad

Fires happen every dry season. Severe, region-wide haze happens when the weather lines up against Southeast Asia, and in 2026 it has.

<Sidenote term="El Niño">A periodic warming of the eastern Pacific Ocean that shifts rainfall away from Indonesia and Australia. It happens every few years. Strong El Niño years (1997, 2015) have been the worst haze years.</Sidenote>

- **A “super” El Niño.** Malaysia’s weather service puts the odds of a very strong El Niño by the end of the year at over 90%, possibly rivalling 1997–98, the worst haze year on record. El Niño pulls rain away from Indonesia, so peat dries out further and fires are harder to put out.
- **A positive Indian Ocean Dipole.** A similar pattern in the Indian Ocean, which also cuts rainfall over Indonesia. When both happen together, the drought is worse.
- **The winds.** From roughly June to September, the southwest monsoon blows from Sumatra towards Singapore and the Malay Peninsula. This October, south-easterly winds have carried smoke from Kalimantan instead. Relief usually comes with the inter-monsoon period in late October and November, when winds weaken and become variable and rain returns.

<Photo file="Borneo fires and smoke, 2002.jpg" alt="Satellite image of fires and smoke over Borneo" caption="Fires (marked in red) and smoke over Borneo, seen by NASA’s Terra satellite in 2002." />

The weather explains the timing. It does not explain the fires. As Shanmugam put it, the haze is “not simply an act of nature”.

## A crisis that keeps coming back

Each episode follows the same pattern: drought, fires, smoke, angry neighbours, promises from Jakarta, a monsoon that brings relief, and a return a few years later.

## Singapore’s playbook at home

Singapore cannot stop the smoke at its source, so at home it prepares in advance and scales up as the PSI rises. A Haze Task Force of 28 government agencies has pre-agreed actions for each band.

<Rows>
  <Row label="Information">Daily NEA advisories with a 24-hour PSI forecast, hourly PM2.5 and activity advice.</Row>
  <Row label="Masks">N95 masks for vulnerable residents above PSI 200, via 37 vending machines; free masks for transport workers and delivery riders.</Row>
  <Row label="Shelters">Air-conditioned haze shelters at community clubs above PSI 201.</Row>
  <Row label="Health costs">Treatment for haze-related conditions capped at S$10 (S$5 for seniors).</Row>
  <Row label="Schools">Lessons and exams continue indoors with air purifiers; closure considered above PSI 300.</Row>
</Rows>

It is typical of how Singapore handles crises: thresholds decided in advance, many agencies coordinated centrally, clear public communication. It keeps daily life running. It can’t make the air clean.

## Dealing with a giant neighbour

The hardest part of the haze for Singapore is not the masks or the shelters. It is that the problem begins in another country, one that is far bigger and that Singapore depends on.

<Sidenote term="Scale">Indonesia has about 280 million people; Singapore about 6 million. Indonesia is the world’s largest archipelagic state and the biggest economy in ASEAN.</Sidenote>

Indonesia is Singapore’s largest neighbour and one of its most important relationships. Singapore is among the biggest foreign investors in Indonesia; the two countries cooperate on defence, trade, tourism and, since 2024, extradition. Relations have been good in recent years. But Indonesia is very sensitive about sovereignty, and about being lectured by a small, rich neighbour. In 2015 its vice-president complained that neighbours never thanked Indonesia for eleven months of clean air. In 2026 an Indonesian official said Singapore’s oxygen comes from Indonesian forests.

Singapore’s strategy this year can be summed up in one move: <mark>separate the companies from the country</mark>. Look at how ministers have spoken:

- **Praise the government.** Shanmugam welcomed Prabowo’s actions: replacing the head of the disaster agency, banning slash-and-burn on peatland, accepting foreign help and making fire prevention a long-term priority. Singapore says its actions against companies are aligned with Indonesia’s own policy.
- **Offer help, don’t impose it.** Grace Fu said Singapore “stands ready to assist where we can”, and waited for Indonesia to say yes. Indonesia only approved foreign assistance on 16 October.
- **Refuse to make it a bilateral dispute.** Asked whether the haze would hurt relations, Shanmugam said: “That’s not the Singapore approach.” He pointed out that Indonesians are suffering from the smoke too.
- **Be blunt about the companies.** The same minister said the haze was “entirely preventable”, driven by companies pursuing profits, and that companies “should not underestimate the Singapore Government’s determination to act.”

The logic is that Singapore cannot order Indonesia to do anything, and a public row would make Jakarta less likely to cooperate. But Singapore does have leverage over companies, because many of the big plantation and paper groups operating in Indonesia are headquartered, listed or financed in Singapore. That leads to the law.

## Can a small country sue a fire?

The **Transboundary Haze Pollution Act** (2014) lets Singapore punish companies, including foreign ones, for conduct abroad that causes haze in Singapore, defined as a 24-hour PSI of 101 or more. Fines run up to S$100,000 a day, capped at S$2 million, and people harmed can sue. To make cases possible, courts can presume ownership from official maps and causation from satellite and weather data.

<Sidenote term="Extraterritorial law">A law that applies to actions outside a country’s borders. Singapore argues international law lets a state protect itself from harm caused within its territory.</Sidenote>

Its record is thin. After 2015, NEA served notices on six companies; only two responded. Two cases were closed in 2017 and four remain open. <mark>No company has ever been prosecuted.</mark> The evidence sits on Indonesian land Singapore can’t inspect, Indonesia won’t publish concession maps, and a warrant only bites if a director visits Singapore.

### What is different in 2026

This time Singapore is adding economic pressure to legal pressure. Beyond notices to ten companies, ordering them to fight fires, stop burning and submit action plans:

<Chain>

1. The Singapore Exchange has queried seven listed agribusinesses, including Wilmar, Golden Agri-Resources and First Resources, about links to the fires.
2. Banks lending to these firms have asked questions, with the Monetary Authority of Singapore supervising how they manage the risk.
3. The government will check whether linked companies receive Singapore grants or incentives, and look past subsidiaries to parent groups and their owners.

</Chain>

A S$2 million fine is small for a big plantation group. Losing access to Singapore’s banks and investors is not. The test is whether the pressure continues after the smoke clears, which is when attention has faded before.

## Malaysia, ASEAN and the region

Malaysia has been hit as hard as Singapore, and in Borneo harder. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim says Prabowo agreed to act, and Malaysia wants a regional mechanism discussed before next month’s ASEAN summit in Manila.

Don’t expect much from ASEAN itself. The “ASEAN Way” relies on consensus and non-interference in members’ affairs, and the 2002 haze treaty has no enforcement mechanism or penalties. ASEAN is useful for sharing data (its weather centre in Singapore tracks hotspots for the region) but cannot make any member act.

Indonesia has a counter-grievance: some companies involved are owned or financed by Malaysians and Singaporeans. That argument has some force, and it is one reason Singapore is now targeting firms tied to its own markets.

## Where reasonable people disagree

<Debate topic="On Singapore’s approach">

  <Side title="Quiet pressure works best">Singapore cannot force a country with 45 times its population to act. Public anger would push Jakarta into a defensive, nationalist position, as in 2016. Praising Prabowo while squeezing companies through finance gives Indonesia room to act and still save face.</Side>

  <Side title="Too soft, too late">Twelve years of the haze law have produced no prosecutions. Notices and stock-exchange queries are easy to ignore. Singapore should name companies, raise penalties sharply and stop treating plantation groups based in its own city as partners while their supply chains burn.</Side>

</Debate>

<Debate topic="On responsibility">

  <Side title="Blame the corporations">Hotspot maps show most fires inside or next to concessions. Large firms have the money and the satellites to stop burning on their land and in their supply chains. If they can’t control fires, they shouldn’t hold the land.</Side>

  <Side title="Blame the system">Small farmers, land disputes, illegal clearing and weak local government all play a part. Focusing only on big firms ignores the poverty that makes fire the cheapest tool, and the neighbours whose demand for palm oil and paper drives the industry.</Side>

## Further context: how Singapore does foreign policy

Singapore became independent in 1965 as a tiny city with no natural resources and two much larger neighbours, one of which, Indonesia, had waged an armed campaign against the Malaysian federation Singapore then belonged to. That shaped a foreign policy built on a few lasting ideas: small states depend on international law and rules; make yourself useful to everyone as a port, financial centre and investor; treat ASEAN as essential even when it is slow; and handle disputes with neighbours through negotiation and courts rather than public confrontation. The haze strains all of these. The 2026 approach, using Singapore’s role as a financial hub as leverage over companies, is the “be useful” principle turned around.
