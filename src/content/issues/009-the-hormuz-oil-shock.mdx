<Overview>

On 28 February 2026 the United States and Israel launched hundreds of air strikes on Iran, killing its Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei. Iran hit back with missiles and drones against Israel, American bases and Gulf states, and within days it did the one thing energy planners had feared for half a century: it shut the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel between Iran and Oman that normally carries about a fifth of the world’s oil and a similar share of its liquefied natural gas. Ship crossings fell from about 138 a day to five or fewer. At the worst point, according to the International Energy Agency (IEA), more than 12 million barrels a day of oil were missing from the market, roughly one barrel in nine. The IEA called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market.

Oil prices jumped from about $72 a barrel when the war began to a peak of about $118 at the end of March. They fell back to about $72 by early July, after a peace memorandum briefly reopened the strait, then climbed again when the truce collapsed in July. Since September Iran has stepped up attacks on tankers, Yemen’s Houthi rebels have opened a second front at the mouth of the Red Sea, and on 26 October Iran’s Revolutionary Guard declared the strait closed again. On 28 October the global benchmark, Brent crude, stood near $115.

The shock has spread far beyond the petrol pump. Diesel, jet fuel, gas, fertiliser and shipping have all become dearer; US inflation has climbed back to 3.7% and the Federal Reserve raised interest rates in September for the first time since 2023. Poorer energy importers in Asia and Africa have rationed fuel and shortened the working week. And yet the world economy has not fallen into recession: the International Monetary Fund (IMF) still expects 3.1% global growth this year. <mark>This issue explains how an oil shock moves through an economy, why this one has hurt less than the 1970s so far, and why the price is unlikely to settle until the strait is reliably open.</mark>

</Overview>

## Why one strait matters so much

<Sidenote term="Strait of Hormuz">The channel between Iran and Oman linking the Persian Gulf to the open ocean. At its narrowest it is about 33 km wide, and the shipping lanes are far narrower. Every tanker leaving Kuwait, Iraq, Qatar, Bahrain and most of Saudi Arabia’s and the UAE’s ports must pass through it.</Sidenote>

The world uses roughly 100 million barrels of oil a day, and in normal times about a fifth of it sails through Hormuz. No other single place matters as much. Saudi Arabia, Iraq, the UAE, Kuwait and Qatar sit behind it, and almost all of their exports leave by sea.

Three features made this closure far worse than a simple loss of one country’s oil.

**First, the spare capacity was trapped behind the same door.** In earlier crises, the world’s safety valve was Saudi Arabia and its neighbours, which keep wells they can switch on quickly when supply falls elsewhere. This time those barrels were stuck behind the strait too. The IEA says effective spare capacity fell by 3.6 million barrels a day, to just 320,000, in March, the lowest on record. Saudi Arabia and the UAE have pipelines that bypass Hormuz, the biggest being Saudi Arabia’s East-West line to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, which can carry about 7 million barrels a day. They have pushed millions of barrels a day through them, but that is a fraction of what normally sails out.

**Second, it was not just crude oil.** Qatar is one of the world’s two biggest exporters of liquefied natural gas (LNG), and Iranian strikes on its Ras Laffan complex cut capacity by about 17%, with repairs estimated at three to five years. Asian spot gas prices rose by more than 140%. About half of the world’s exports of urea, a nitrogen fertiliser, and of sulphur pass through Hormuz too, so fertiliser prices rose by up to 40% in the first three weeks. Qatar also produces about a third of the world’s helium, used in chip-making and hospital scanners. Reuters notes that, unlike in the 1970s, this crisis hit crude, gas, refined fuels and fertiliser at the same time.

**Third, the shipping system itself broke.** Even when the strait was nominally open, few shipowners would risk it. The cost of hiring a supertanker went from about $65,000 a day to $230,000, and shipping one cargo of American crude to Asia cost about $77 million in early October, against an average of $9.2 million last year, according to Bloomberg, citing the Baltic Exchange. From 28 September to 4 October fewer than 23 ships a day crossed the strait, where hundreds had crossed before the war.

### What has kept oil flowing at all

The world has not lost 12 million barrels a day for eight months. Several things have filled part of the gap:

- **Emergency stocks.** IEA members pledged 400 million barrels in March, by far the largest release in the agency’s history (the previous record, in 2022, was 120 million). The Council on Foreign Relations puts the stock released through coordinated action at about 273 million barrels. Japan alone began releasing 80 million barrels, about 15 days of its demand. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve has fallen to its lowest level since 1982.
- **Pipelines and partial reopenings.** Gulf producers have diverted oil through bypass pipelines, and when the strait was partly open, exports recovered fast: the IEA says Gulf crude exports rose by 4.4 million barrels a day in September alone, to 13.9 million, more than three-quarters of pre-war levels. The US military says its naval escorts have moved over 1.5 billion barrels through the strait since June.
- **Less demand.** China cut its seaborne crude imports by more than 5 million barrels a day in the first half of the year, more than 40% below pre-war levels, by drawing on reserves, burning more of its own coal, banning fuel exports and accelerating electric cars. Higher prices have done the rest. The World Bank expects world oil use to fall 2.5% this year, a drop exceeded only in 1980 and 2020.

But these cushions are being used up. Global oil stocks were 509 million barrels lower than at the end of February by the latest count in the IEA’s October report, and emergency reserves that have been drawn down cannot be drawn down twice.

## How an oil shock travels through an economy

<Sidenote term="Supply shock">An event that reduces how much an economy can produce, pushing prices up and output down at the same time. A demand shock moves both in the same direction. Supply shocks are hard for central banks because fighting the inflation also deepens the slowdown.</Sidenote>

Economists call a sudden rise in energy prices a textbook negative supply shock. The IMF’s chief economist, Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, described in April how it spreads, and the past eight months have followed his script.

<Chain>

1. **Crude becomes fuel.** Refiners pay more for crude and charge more for petrol, diesel and jet fuel. Diesel has risen most, and trucks, ships, farms and factories cannot easily do without it. US diesel was $6.28 a gallon on 8 October, up almost 70% since the war began; the IEA expects diesel to stay tight into the second half of 2027.
2. **Fuel becomes everything else.** Transport costs feed into food, goods and travel. Fertiliser and gas costs feed into next year’s harvests and electricity bills. Airlines raise fares or fail: the US low-cost carrier Spirit Airlines stopped flying in May, citing fuel costs.
3. **Prices rise faster.** Energy goes straight into headline inflation. In the US, petrol alone accounted for more than half of September’s monthly rise in consumer prices.
4. **People try to catch up.** This is the dangerous step. If workers demand higher pay and firms raise prices to protect their margins, a one-off jump in prices turns into a lasting rise in inflation, which economists call second-round effects.
5. **Central banks respond.** To stop that, central banks may raise interest rates even as the economy slows. The European Central Bank raised rates in June and September; the Fed raised them in September.
6. **Spending falls.** Households spend more on fuel and less on everything else; borrowing costs rise; confidence drops. Output slows.

</Chain>

<mark>The size of the damage depends less on how high oil goes than on how long it stays there, and on whether people start expecting high inflation to last.</mark> In April the IMF sketched three paths for this year. If the conflict was short, world growth would be 3.1% and inflation 4.4%. If energy prices rose more sharply and expectations slipped, growth would fall to 2.5% and inflation rise to 5.4%. In a severe case, with disruption lasting into 2027 and markets tightening sharply, growth would fall to 2% this year and next, and inflation would go above 6%. Its October forecast, 3.1% growth and 4.7% inflation, sits between the first two: the economy has held up better than feared, but prices have been worse.

### Why importers in poorer countries suffer twice

Oil is priced in US dollars. When a war scares investors, they tend to pull money out of riskier economies and into the dollar, so many importers’ currencies fall just as oil rises, and they pay more twice over. The Philippine peso hit a record low in September, and the Japanese yen fell to a 40-year low this year. The IMF says the hardest-hit economies are energy importers with thin buffers, and that higher yields, currency falls and capital outflows have amplified the shock in emerging Asia.

## 1973, 1990, 2022: how this compares

<Sidenote term="Real (inflation-adjusted) price">A price measured in today’s money. $12 a barrel in 1974 was a much bigger share of incomes than $12 today, so comparisons across decades use real prices.</Sidenote>

By the amount of oil lost each day, this is the biggest shock on record. Reuters, using IEA figures, compares the peak daily losses:

<Rows>
  <Row label="1973–74: the Arab oil embargo">Arab producers cut output and embargoed the US and others over support for Israel in the Yom Kippur War. Peak loss: 4.5 million barrels a day. Oil roughly quadrupled, from about $3 to nearly $12 a barrel, and the world fell into its deepest recession since the 1930s while inflation soared.</Row>
  <Row label="1978–79: the Iranian revolution">Iran’s output collapsed. Peak loss: 5.6 million barrels a day. Because it lasted years, its total loss, over 4 billion barrels by one US estimate, dwarfs today’s so far.</Row>
  <Row label="1990–91: Iraq invades Kuwait">Peak loss: about 4.3 million barrels a day. Saudi Arabia and others pumped more, and the US-led war ended fears of a long cut-off. It still helped push the US into recession.</Row>
  <Row label="2022: Russia invades Ukraine">Russian oil output fell only about 1 million barrels a day, but Europe lost most of its Russian gas. European gas prices rose to about 25 times their level of two years earlier, and governments spent hundreds of billions on support.</Row>
  <Row label="2026: the Iran war">Peak loss: more than 12 million barrels a day, plus a fifth of the world’s LNG, plus fertiliser and helium. Reuters calculated a cumulative loss of 624 million barrels in the first 52 days alone, similar to the whole Arab embargo.</Row>

<Quote cite="The IEA, as reported by Reuters, April 2026">The worst energy crisis the world has faced.</Quote>

So why has the economic damage been smaller than in the 1970s, at least so far? Vijay Vaitheeswaran of the Council on Foreign Relations points to several reasons. Oil markets were well supplied when the war began: the IEA had expected a surplus of nearly 4 million barrels a day this year. Strategic reserves, which did not exist in 1973 and were created because of it, could be released. The US has gone from an anxious importer to an exporter. And adjusted for inflation, oil prices remain well below the peaks of past crises. Economies also use much less oil for each dollar of output than they did 50 years ago. Vaitheeswaran also argues that China’s calm, planned cuts in demand mattered as much as any increase in supply.

## Why prices won’t settle

<WhyList>
<Why title="The physical damage takes years to repair">

Strikes have hit Ras Laffan, Ras Tanura and other Gulf facilities. Qatar’s lost gas capacity may take three to five years to restore. Even a full peace deal would not bring all of the supply back at once.

</Why>
<Why title="Both sides are blockading">

For most of the time since April the strait has been squeezed from both ends: Iran attacks or threatens ships it does not approve of, and the US Navy blockades ships going to or from Iranian ports. The US military says its blockade has kept $25 billion of Iranian crude off the market. Each side’s main lever is the other side’s main grievance, and the latest Qatari draft still leaves them far apart on shipping and sanctions.

</Why>
<Why title="The war has no clear end point">

The US named several goals at the start, from preventing an Iranian nuclear weapon to changing Iran’s government. “The key problem is a lack of tangible goals in this war,” an Oxford energy security expert, Adi Imsirovic, told Al Jazeera in March. A war without an agreed finish line is hard for traders to price, so every report of strikes or talks moves the market. The April truce broke down within days; the June memorandum lasted three weeks.

</Why>
<Why title="A second chokepoint has opened">

Since September the Houthis have seized territory around the Bab al-Mandab strait at the southern end of the Red Sea, attacked Saudi airports and, according to The National, struck Saudi Arabia again in late October. Oil from Saudi Arabia’s bypass pipeline is loaded at Yanbu on the Red Sea, and the route from there to Asia runs through the Bab al-Mandab, so the Gulf’s main detour is now under threat too.

</Why>
<Why title="The cushions are wearing thin">

Emergency stocks, inventories and spare refining capacity absorbed the first months. Stocks are now hundreds of millions of barrels lower, reserves need refilling, and the IEA warned in October that renewed attacks leave the recovery of Gulf exports fragile.

</Why>
<Why title="Politics adds its own premium">

Trump said in September that prices would fall “right after” the midterms, and in October that he would not attack Iran before 3 November. The National reports that Washington has told Gulf states to prepare for a possible renewed US campaign after the election. Markets have read the pledges as a pause, not a peace: Brent rose 4% on the day of Trump’s October post.

</Why>
</WhyList>

<Answer title="So is it the war, the markets or the politics?">

Mainly the war, and specifically the strait. Speculation and fear move prices day to day, but the underlying problem is physical: the world’s spare oil is trapped behind a channel that both sides are using as a weapon, and the reserves that softened the first blow are partly spent.

That is why prices fell to $72 within two weeks of the June memorandum and climbed back above $100 within weeks of its collapse. Until there is an agreement that both Iran and the US expect to hold, and until shipowners and insurers believe it, oil will carry a large war premium. Political promises about when prices will fall cannot change that.

</Answer>

## The faster turn away from oil

Every oil shock changes how the world uses energy. The 1970s gave the world fuel-efficiency rules, strategic reserves, France’s nuclear programme and a shift to smaller Japanese cars. This one is speeding up the move to electricity.

- **Electric cars.** Electric vehicles reached 26.1% of global new-car sales in May, a record and the first month above a quarter, according to Goldman Sachs. That is 3.4 percentage points higher than before the shock. China accounts for most of the jump: nearly 63% of its retail car sales in May were electric. In the US, where the federal tax credit expired in 2025, the share barely moved.
- **Solar and batteries.** Chinese solar panel exports rose more than 80% year on year in March, according to the think tank Ember. NPR reports that countries across Asia and Africa are installing solar, batteries and electric vehicles to cut fuel imports: the Philippines imported more than $400 million of solar panels between February and May, and Pakistan’s solar boom has let it cut oil and gas imports.

The scale is still modest. Goldman estimates the extra electric cars will cut oil demand by only 130,000 to 320,000 barrels a day by the end of 2027, compared with world use of about 100 million. The shift is real but slow; it makes the next shock smaller rather than this one shorter.

## What this means for policymakers

An oil shock gives governments a set of problems with no clean answers. The tools exist, but each has a cost, and most of what matters, above all reopening the strait, is beyond any single government’s control.

### The central bank’s dilemma

<Sidenote term="Looking through">When a central bank does not respond to a rise in inflation it expects to be temporary, such as a jump in oil prices, to avoid slowing the economy for no lasting gain.</Sidenote>

Central banks usually “look through” an oil shock: raising interest rates cannot produce more oil, and higher rates on top of high fuel prices deepen the slowdown. But the IMF warns that looking through is safe only while people still expect inflation to come back down. After the inflation surge of 2021–22, that trust is thinner, and the IMF thinks second-round effects could be larger this time.

That is the argument now dividing the Fed. It raised rates unanimously in September, the first rise since 2023. On 28 October it held them, but two officials, Governor Lisa Cook and the Cleveland Fed’s Beth Hammack, voted for another rise. The chair, Kevin Warsh, said “inflation is too high” and that energy prices “have moved higher again in recent weeks”, and said politics “played no role” in a decision taken days before the midterms. The ECB has raised rates twice. The risk on one side is a 1970s-style spiral; on the other, raising rates into a supply shock that ends with a peace deal, and causing a recession for nothing.

### The subsidy trap

Faced with angry drivers, most governments reach for price caps, tax cuts and subsidies. Japan has spent about ¥9 trillion holding petrol prices down; India and Germany cut fuel taxes; Thailand froze diesel prices; Indonesia’s fuel and energy subsidy bill roughly doubled. These are popular and quick, but the IMF says they are expensive and badly aimed: in its October outlook it estimates that measures that hold prices down can cost 3 to 22 times as much as targeted, temporary payments to the households that need them most. They also blunt the price signal that persuades people to use less fuel, which keeps world prices higher for everyone, and export bans push shortages onto other countries. The IMF and World Bank both say to protect the poorest directly and let prices do their work, advice that is hardest to follow exactly when governments are most unpopular.

### Stocks, supply and the long game

<Rows>
  <Row label="Emergency stocks">Releasing them softened the first blow. But they now need refilling, and buying back hundreds of millions of barrels will itself support prices. Countries with little or no stock, such as Pakistan, had nothing to release at all.</Row>
  <Row label="Alternative supply">The US temporarily allowed purchases of sanctioned Russian oil in March, and on 9 October Trump announced a deal for Russia to supply diesel, which Ukraine’s president condemned. Such deals trade one foreign-policy goal for another, and analysts doubt this one will move prices much.</Row>
  <Row label="Using less">China’s demand cuts, and the faster spread of electric vehicles and solar, show that demand can respond. Policies that speed this up, from efficiency rules to public transport, pay off over years, not weeks.</Row>
  <Row label="Help for the poorest countries">The World Bank says it has $50 to $60 billion available through existing instruments, and the IMF is bringing forward or discussing new lending for countries such as Ethiopia and Bangladesh. Food insecurity is the biggest risk: the World Bank’s adverse case has food prices above their 2022 peak.</Row>
  <Row label="Ending the war">The one measure that would solve the problem. It depends on the US and Iran, and on mediators such as Qatar, Oman and Pakistan, not on energy policy at all.</Row>
</Rows>

<Answer title="So what can governments actually do?">

Soften the blow, not remove it. Central banks can keep inflation from becoming entrenched, at the cost of slower growth. Finance ministries can protect the poorest households, though cheaper blanket subsidies are more popular. Energy ministries can release stocks and cut demand. None of them can produce the 10-plus million barrels a day that sit behind the Strait of Hormuz. That depends on diplomacy.

</Answer>

## Where reasonable people disagree

<Debate topic="On whether central banks should raise rates in response">
  <Side title="Raise rates now">Inflation was already above target before the war, and the memory of 2021–22, when central banks called inflation “transitory” and were wrong, is fresh. If people come to expect high inflation, it becomes self-fulfilling and much more costly to reverse. September’s US inflation figure of 3.7% shows the shock spreading, and a central bank that waits risks having to raise rates far more later.</Side>
  <Side title="Look through it">Higher rates cannot reopen the Strait of Hormuz. They hit households and firms already squeezed by fuel costs, and if a peace deal arrives, oil could fall as quickly as it did in June, leaving rates too high in a slowing economy. Core inflation in the US, which strips out energy and food, was 2.6% in September, suggesting the shock has not yet spread widely.</Side>
</Debate>

<Debate topic="On whether this shock will speed the end of the oil age">
  <Side title="Yes, this is the turning point">Shocks change behaviour permanently. The 1970s created the IEA, fuel standards and nuclear power; 2022 pushed Europe off Russian gas. Now record electric car sales in China, a solar boom across Asia and Africa, and governments that have watched fuel run out will make energy security mean electricity they generate at home. Oil-importing countries have every reason never to be this exposed again.</Side>
  <Side title="No, it will entrench oil">Most of the response so far has been more drilling, Russian supply deals, coal and fuel subsidies, not clean energy. Goldman estimates the extra electric cars will save less than a third of a million barrels a day by 2027. And when the strait reopens, cheaper oil will weaken the urge to switch. History shows demand falls during shocks but recovers afterwards.</Side>
</Debate>

## Further context: the oil weapon

The idea that oil could be used as a weapon dates back to at least 1973, when Arab producers led by Saudi Arabia cut output and embargoed the US and the Netherlands over their support for Israel. Petrol queues, Sunday driving bans and rationing followed across the West. The response shaped the energy system that is being tested today: the International Energy Agency was founded in 1974 to coordinate oil stocks among importing countries, the US created its Strategic Petroleum Reserve, and countries set fuel-efficiency rules and built nuclear plants.

Iran has threatened to close Hormuz many times since the 1980s, when it and Iraq attacked each other’s tankers in the “tanker war”. Until 2026 it never did, because closing the strait would also stop its own exports and invite a military response. This year its Supreme Leader had just been killed and the country was under heavy attack, which left the strait as one of the few levers it had. Its new Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, called the threat to shipping a “lever” that “must continue to be used”.

## Key terms

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Brent crude">The main global benchmark price for oil, based on crude from the North Sea. Most of the world’s oil is priced off it.</Term>
  <Term name="Strait of Hormuz">The channel between Iran and Oman through which about a fifth of the world’s oil and LNG normally passes.</Term>
  <Term name="Spare capacity">Oil production that can be switched on quickly, mostly held by Saudi Arabia and the UAE. It is the market’s usual shock absorber.</Term>
  <Term name="Strategic reserve">A government stockpile of crude or fuel kept for emergencies. IEA members must hold stocks equal to 90 days of net imports.</Term>
  <Term name="LNG">Liquefied natural gas: gas cooled to a liquid so it can be shipped by tanker. Qatar is one of the two biggest exporters.</Term>
  <Term name="Supply shock">A sudden fall in the supply of something essential, which raises prices and lowers output at the same time.</Term>
  <Term name="Second-round effects">When higher energy prices lead to higher wages and prices elsewhere, turning a one-off jump into lasting inflation.</Term>
  <Term name="Stagflation">Stagnant growth combined with high inflation, the signature of the 1970s oil shocks.</Term>
  <Term name="Core inflation">Inflation excluding food and energy, used to judge whether price rises are spreading.</Term>
  <Term name="War-risk premium">The extra price traders and insurers charge because of the risk of attacks or closure, on top of the cost of the oil itself.</Term>
</KeyTerms>
