<Overview>

Since January 2026, students in Singapore’s secondary schools may not use smartphones or smartwatches at any point in the school day: not in lessons, not at recess, not during sports or clubs. Devices stay in lockers or bags. Primary schools have had the same rule since January 2025. The Ministry of Education (MOE) said it was acting on research linking heavy screen use to less sleep, less exercise and less time with friends face to face, and a government survey had found that about a quarter of 13- to 17-year-olds spent more than four hours a day on digital devices on weekdays, outside schoolwork.

Now the government has turned to the companies behind the screens. At the National Day Rally in August, Prime Minister Lawrence Wong said that one in six young people aged 10 to 24 showed signs of problematic social media use, and that letting children declare their own age “is not working well”. Platforms will have to run “robust and reliable” age checks and build in stronger safeguards for teenagers, he said, or face a higher minimum age. A law is reportedly planned for early 2027.

Singapore is part of a global wave. Australia banned social media accounts for under-16s in December 2025, and the share of countries with school phone bans has more than doubled since 2023. <mark>Yet the research is far less settled than the politics: scientists still disagree about how much phones and social media harm teenagers, and the best-known study of school bans found no clear benefit.</mark> This issue explains what the rules do, what the evidence shows, why governments moved anyway, and whether restricting access or changing how apps are designed is the better lever.

</Overview>

## What exactly do the school rules say?

Before 2026, Singapore’s secondary students could not use phones during lessons but could use them at recess and in co-curricular activities (CCAs), the sports, uniformed groups and clubs most students join. From January, the ban covers the whole school day, including recess, CCAs and supplementary, enrichment and remedial lessons. Students can still bring phones to school, but must keep them in lockers or school bags. Smartwatches are included because they allow messaging and apps.

The rules have some give. “Where necessary, schools may allow students to use smartphones by exception,” MOE said, and each school enforces the ban under its own discipline policy. Students at junior colleges and Millennia Institute, the pre-university level, are expected to manage their own use, though they still need a teacher’s permission to use phones in lessons.

<Sidenote term="Personal learning device">The laptop or tablet, usually an iPad or Chromebook, that every Singapore secondary student has owned for schoolwork since 2021. MOE controls its settings.</Sidenote>

A second change got less attention. Every secondary student has a school-issued personal learning device, and from January those devices go to sleep from 10.30pm to 6.30am, half an hour earlier than before.

The ban is part of Grow Well SG, the government’s plan for children’s health. In January 2025 the health ministry tightened its screen-time guidelines: no screen time for children under 18 months, and under two hours a day outside schoolwork for children aged 7 to 12. There is no official limit for teenagers. Alongside the school rules, two ministries announced digital parenting programmes with community groups.

Minister of State for Education Jasmin Lau explained the move as a parent. “As a parent, I am concerned about the environment our children grow up in,” she wrote on Instagram, adding that “we need to find a healthy balance and recognise that habits form early.” A ministry spokesman said some secondary schools had already adopted the tighter rules and seen better wellbeing, focus and more physical interaction at breaks.

## How much time do teenagers spend on screens?

Singapore’s figures are similar to those in other rich countries. The September 2025 government survey found that about a quarter of teenagers aged 13 to 17 spent more than four hours a day on digital devices on weekdays, not counting schoolwork. At weekends the share rose to nearly half.

In his August speech, the Prime Minister went further. “Today, one in six young people aged 10 to 24 in Singapore show signs of problematic social media use,” he said. “For some the problem can be severe: they spend six or seven hours a day on social media, sometimes till the wee hours of the morning.” He added that although most platforms set a minimum age of 13 on paper, “when I visit primary schools, I am struck by how many children are already active on social media.”

<mark>Most of that time is spent outside school.</mark> A school ban removes phones for roughly seven hours on school days; it does nothing about evenings, weekends and holidays, which is why the government has turned to the platforms themselves.

## What does the evidence actually show?

<Sidenote term="Correlation and causation">Two things can rise together (correlation) without one causing the other (causation). Teenagers who are anxious may use social media more, rather than social media making them anxious, or a third factor may drive both.</Sidenote>

The debate is dominated by one book and its critics.

### The case against phones

In *The Anxious Generation* (2024), Jonathan Haidt, a social psychologist at New York University, argues that childhood was “rewired” around 2010, when smartphones and social media spread among teenagers. He points to a sudden rise in anxiety, depression, self-harm and suicide among young people, especially girls, in the US and other rich countries. According to figures from Haidt and his researcher Zach Rausch, the share of American teenagers reporting a major depressive episode in the past year rose by more than 150% after 2010, and among girls aged 10 to 14, emergency room visits for self-harm rose 188% and suicides 167%.

Haidt names four harms: social deprivation (less time with friends in person), sleep deprivation, fragmented attention and addiction. His proposals include phone-free schools, raising the minimum age for social media accounts to 16, giving children basic phones before smartphones, and more independence and free play. The book spent more than 100 weeks on the New York Times bestseller list, and according to Reuters it helped spur Australia’s ban: the premier of South Australia acted after his wife asked him to read it.

### The case for caution

Many researchers who study the subject say the evidence does not support such a strong claim. Candice Odgers, a developmental psychologist at the University of California, Irvine, argued in a review in the journal *Nature* that most research finds no large, consistent negative effect of social media on young people’s mental health, and that the causation may run the other way: teenagers who are already struggling may use social media more. She said Haidt was adding to a “rising hysteria” and “telling stories that are unsupported by research”. Andrew Przybylski of the University of Oxford put it this way: “Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. Right now, I’d argue he doesn’t have that.”

The critics make several specific points:

- **The links are real but small.** Haidt himself accepts a correlation of about 0.15 between social media use and poor mental health for girls. That is a modest relationship: most of the difference between happier and unhappier teenagers lies elsewhere.
- **The trends differ by country and measure.** The journalist David Wallace-Wells noted that some of the rise in recorded self-harm may reflect changes in how hospitals screen for and code it, and that measures of life satisfaction have not fallen in the same way.
- **Other explanations exist.** Odgers warned that blaming social media may distract from the real causes of distress, and the psychologist Chris Ferguson noted that the rise in US suicides is not specific to teenagers.

The disagreement is narrower than it sounds. Rausch called it “a normal academic debate” and said he was “totally open to the idea that maybe we’re somewhat wrong about just how much it can explain.” Few on either side deny that the trends in teenage distress are real. The real dispute is over how big the effect is, whether phones are the main cause of the rise in distress, and therefore whether sweeping bans are justified.

### The study of school bans

<Sidenote term="Cross-sectional study">A study that compares groups at a single point in time. It can show whether two things go together but not which causes which.</Sidenote>

The most-cited study of school phone rules, the SMART Schools study led by Victoria Goodyear at the University of Birmingham and published in *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe* in February 2025, compared pupils aged 12 to 15 at 30 English secondary schools: 20 with restrictive phone policies and 10 with permissive ones. It concluded there was “no evidence to support that restrictive school phone policies, in their current forms, have a beneficial effect on adolescents’ mental health and wellbeing or related outcomes.” Pupils at stricter schools used their phones less during the school day, but their overall phone and social media use was no different. The authors also found that heavier phone and social media use went with worse outcomes, which they said showed “the need to address phone and social media use in adolescents” beyond the school gate.

Haidt and his colleagues published a detailed critique. They pointed out that the study is cross-sectional, so it cannot show cause and effect; that 9 of the 10 “permissive” schools still banned phones in lessons, while only 4 of the 20 “restrictive” schools were truly phone-free, so the study mostly compared classroom bans with bag-and-locker rules; and that academic performance was measured by a single teacher rating rather than test scores. Permissive schools are so rare in England that the researchers found only 96 among 1,341 they screened.

<mark>So the honest summary is that a school ban reliably reduces phone use at school, but there is not yet good evidence that it improves wellbeing or grades on its own.</mark> Teachers, though, are firmly in favour: a 2024 survey by the US National Education Association found 90% of American teachers supported banning phones during lessons.

## Will Singapore’s school ban work?

As a rule about school hours, probably yes: the evidence from England and elsewhere is that bans do cut phone use during the school day. The harder questions are about enforcement and what happens outside school.

When the ban was announced, parents interviewed by CNA largely supported it, and some called it overdue. But many doubted it could be enforced. “Who’s going to be in charge to ensure that their phones are off or in the locker?” asked Kelvin Choo, a father of five. A teacher with more than ten years’ experience said: “Teachers can’t be everywhere all at once.” Students can still reach messaging apps through web browsers on their school-issued devices, and some teachers let students use phones in class when they have forgotten to charge those devices.

Michelle Goh, a parent, summed up the limit: “It will solve part of the problem, but not the whole problem.” Professor Michael Chia of the National Institute of Education, who studies screen time, said the measures could help “rebalance screen time versus the rest of the time.”

The SMART Schools finding matters here: if pupils make up the lost time after school, a ban changes where they use phones more than how much. That is the gap the government is now trying to close.

## Why is the government now going after the platforms?

Because most screen time happens at home, and because the platforms control the design choices that keep teenagers scrolling.

<Sidenote term="Age assurance">Methods of checking or estimating a user’s age, from asking for a date of birth (self-declaration) to checking government ID or estimating age from a face scan. Stronger methods are more accurate but raise privacy concerns.</Sidenote>

The Prime Minister’s argument in August was that the age limit on social media barely exists in practice. “On paper, most social media platforms already set a minimum age of 13,” he said. “But enforcement is largely left to the platforms themselves. And today they mostly rely on users to declare their own ages.” His plan has two parts:

<Rows>
  <Row label="Real age checks">“Self-declaration is just not good enough,” Wong said. Platforms “will have to put in place robust and reliable checks, so that the minimum age is properly set and enforced.” Singapore already requires app stores to check ages: according to Wikipedia’s summary of the rules, since April 2026 app stores have had to stop users estimated to be under 18 from downloading unsuitable apps.</Row>
  <Row label="Safer design for teenagers">For users aged 13 and over, platforms will have to build in stronger safeguards. In a later interview, Josephine Teo reportedly named features such as infinite scrolling, autoplay and contact from strangers, and said a daily time limit for teenagers was being seriously considered, suggesting three hours was too long.</Row>
  <Row label="A stick for platforms that refuse">“If its safeguards remain inadequate, we will consider additional restrictions, including raising the minimum age for access to that platform beyond 13,” Wong said. Teo reportedly put the figure at 18 for any platform unwilling or unable to protect 13- to 17-year-olds, with a law planned for early 2027.</Row>
</Rows>

This is a deliberately different route from Australia’s. Rather than a blanket ban for under-16s, Singapore says it will set a minimum standard of design and raise the age only for platforms that fail it. It builds on the Online Safety (Relief and Accountability) Act, passed in November 2025 under Teo, which set up a government commission to help victims of online harms.

### What Australia’s ban shows so far

<Sidenote term="eSafety Commissioner">Australia’s online safety regulator, which enforces the under-16 ban and can fine platforms that fail to take reasonable steps to keep children off.</Sidenote>

Australia’s law, in force since 10 December 2025, requires platforms including Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, X, YouTube and Reddit to take “reasonable steps” to stop under-16s holding accounts, with fines of up to A$49.5 million. Messaging and gaming services such as WhatsApp and Roblox are not covered, and children and parents face no penalties.

The early record is mixed. Platforms reported removing or restricting millions of accounts, and by March 2026 more than 310,000 further accounts had been deactivated. But the eSafety Commissioner reported that 7 in 10 children still held accounts on restricted platforms when the ban began, and media reports since have described children getting around age checks or creating new accounts. In June 2026 the government proposed doubling the maximum fine to A$99 million and extending age checks to app stores. A digital rights group has challenged the law in Australia’s High Court.

## Why did governments turn against phones all at once?

<WhyList>
<Why title="Teenage distress rose, and phones were the obvious suspect">

Measures of anxiety, depression and self-harm among young people rose sharply in many rich countries in the 2010s, in the same years smartphones and social media spread. Whatever the true share of blame, the timing made phones the most visible explanation, and parents could see the change in their own homes.

</Why>
<Why title="A persuasive book gave the worry a story and a plan">

*The Anxious Generation* turned scattered research into a clear narrative with four simple rules. Politicians could cite it, parents could act on it, and in Australia it directly prompted a state premier to act. Research that says “the effects are small and mixed” is much harder to campaign on.

</Why>
<Why title="The platforms’ business model works against teenagers">

Social media companies earn money from attention, so features like infinite scroll, autoplay and constant notifications are designed to keep people using the apps. Internal documents leaked from Facebook in 2021 showed the company knew its products could harm some teenage girls. Governments that once trusted platforms to police themselves, including by letting children declare their own age, no longer do.

</Why>
<Why title="Each family alone faces a collective action problem">

<Sidenote term="Collective action problem">A situation where everyone would be better off if all acted together, but no one wants to act alone. No parent wants their child to be the only one without a phone or social media.</Sidenote>

Many parents would like to delay phones and social media, but no one wants their child to be the only one left out of the class group chat. A rule that applies to everyone, at school or by law, solves that problem in a way individual parenting cannot. This is one reason school bans are popular even when their measured effects are small.

</Why>
<Why title="School bans are cheap and visible">

A school phone ban costs a government almost nothing, applies immediately and is easy to explain. Regulating global platforms is slow and contested. It is no surprise that school bans came first, and that the share of countries with them rose from 23% in 2023 to 40% in 2024, according to UNESCO figures, and to more than half since.

</Why>
</WhyList>

<Answer title="So is it evidence, moral panic or politics?">

Some of each. There is real evidence that heavy social media use goes with worse sleep and wellbeing, especially for younger teenagers, and teachers and parents report that phones disrupt school life. But the strong claim that phones caused a mental health epidemic is contested, and there is little evidence yet that school bans improve wellbeing on their own.

Governments moved anyway because the costs of waiting for proof seemed higher than the costs of acting, because school rules are cheap and popular, and because platforms had lost the benefit of the doubt. Singapore’s turn from school rules to platform design is, in part, an admission that a school ban alone was never going to be enough.

</Answer>

## What can Singapore’s government do?

The policy problem is that most teenage screen time happens in places the state does not control, on apps built by foreign companies whose business depends on attention. The government has three broad tools, each with trade-offs.

### Rules in schools

The school ban is in place and cheap to keep. Its costs fall mainly on teachers, who must enforce it, and on families who rely on phones to reach their children, which is why schools can grant exceptions. The open question is whether schools will enforce it consistently, and whether the government will measure its effects rather than assume them. The English study suggests looking at total screen time, sleep and wellbeing, not just at whether phones stay in lockers.

### Age checks

Making platforms verify ages is the heart of the new plan, and the hardest part to get right.

- **Privacy:** reliable checks mean collecting ID or face scans from millions of users, adults included, creating new data that can leak or be misused.
- **Accuracy:** age estimation from faces is least accurate around the ages that matter, such as 13 and 16.
- **Circumvention:** teenagers can borrow a parent’s account, use a VPN to appear to be abroad, or move to smaller apps that are not covered. Australia’s experience shows how many slip through.
- **Reach:** Singapore is a small market. Platforms may comply because the cost is low, but a small country has less leverage than the EU or the US if a platform simply decides to restrict its service.

### Safer design

Requiring platforms to change how their apps work for teenagers, by limiting infinite scroll, autoplay and contact from strangers, or setting daily time limits, targets the mechanism critics most worry about without cutting teenagers off entirely. But it is new territory: regulators must define and test “adequate” safeguards, platforms will resist changes that reduce use, and no one yet knows which design changes make the biggest difference. Threatening to raise the age to 18 for platforms that refuse gives the government leverage, but it would cut off older teenagers who use social media for friendships, news and school.

<Answer title="So what can the government actually do?">

It can keep phones out of classrooms, make platforms prove ages and redesign their apps for teenagers, and back parents with guidance. What it cannot do alone is make global platforms change their business model, or guarantee that age checks will not be dodged. The test of the 2027 law will be whether it sets clear, measurable standards for design, and whether the government measures what happens to teenagers’ sleep and wellbeing rather than just counting phones in lockers.

</Answer>

## Where do reasonable people disagree?

<Debate topic="On whether restricting phones is good policy">
  <Side title="Yes, act now">The harms most researchers accept, lost sleep, distraction and less time with friends, are real, and children cannot wait a decade for perfect studies. School bans cost almost nothing, teachers overwhelmingly support them, and they solve a collective action problem no family can solve alone. If the effects turn out to be small, little is lost; if they are large, waiting would have been costly.</Side>
  <Side title="No, it treats a symptom">The best study of school bans found no measurable benefit for wellbeing, and teenagers make up the time after school. Blanket rules risk punishing the many teenagers who use phones well, cutting off isolated young people from support online, and distracting from other causes of distress, from academic pressure to too few mental health services. Policy should follow the evidence, not a bestseller.</Side>
</Debate>

<Debate topic="On whether to ban by age or regulate design">
  <Side title="Set a firm age limit">Age limits are simple, easy to explain and give parents a clear rule to point to. Asking platforms to make their apps less addictive invites endless negotiation, and companies whose income depends on attention will always find ways to keep teenagers scrolling. A clear line, as Australia drew at 16, changes social norms in a way design tweaks cannot.</Side>
  <Side title="Make the apps safer">Australia’s experience shows age bans leak, and they push teenagers to less regulated corners of the internet without teaching them to use social media well. Changing the features that drive compulsive use, such as infinite scroll, autoplay and contact from strangers, protects teenagers while keeping the benefits of being online. Singapore’s plan to raise the age only for platforms that refuse gives companies a reason to change.</Side>
</Debate>
