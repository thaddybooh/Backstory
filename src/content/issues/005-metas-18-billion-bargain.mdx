<Overview>

On 26 August 2026, eight days into a trial in a federal courtroom in Oakland, California, Meta, the company that owns Instagram, Facebook and WhatsApp, settled a lawsuit brought by a coalition of US states. The states accused Meta of deliberately designing Instagram and Facebook to hook children, of hiding what it knew about the harm, and of collecting data from children under 13 without their parents’ permission. Meta agreed to pay up to about $18 billion over ten years, the biggest settlement of its kind against a technology company. More than 50 state and territorial attorneys general, Republican and Democrat, signed on. Meta denies wrongdoing and admitted none.

The money is not the most unusual part. Meta also agreed to change its products for every American user under 18. Within months, teenagers’ Instagram and Facebook accounts will by default be limited to two hours a day across both apps, locked from midnight to 6am, and silenced during school hours, with only a parent able to lift the limits. Like counts will be hidden from teens, beauty filters that mimic cosmetic surgery banned for them, and an independent auditor will check that Meta complies. If TikTok, YouTube and Snapchat adopt the same rules, Meta pays about $5 billion more and its limit tightens to one hour.

The deal came after a run of courtroom defeats. In March a Los Angeles jury found Meta and YouTube negligent for designing apps that harmed a young woman who started using them as a child, the first verdict of its kind; a New Mexico jury found that Meta had misled the public about child safety. For decades, a 1996 law known as Section 230 meant platforms could rarely be sued over what happened on them. These cases got around it by treating apps as products with design defects, the way earlier lawsuits treated cigarettes and opioid painkillers. This issue explains how that happened, why Meta chose to settle, what changes for teenagers, and why, with Congress unable to pass a law, judges and state lawyers are now setting the rules for one of the world’s biggest industries.

</Overview>

## What Meta agreed to

The settlement has two halves: money, and rules for the product. The rules are what make it unusual, because they change what tens of millions of American teenagers see and how long they can see it.

### The product changes

According to the California attorney general’s office, which co-led the case, the deal requires Meta to do the following for users under 18:

<Rows>
  <Row label="Time limit">A default limit of two hours a day, counted across Instagram and Facebook combined. Only a parent can lift it. It drops to one hour if rival platforms agree to similar terms.</Row>
  <Row label="Night block">The apps lock by default from midnight to 6am, again liftable only by a parent. The window widens to 10pm–7am if rivals join.</Row>
  <Row label="Quiet hours">No notifications from 10pm to 7am, or during the school day (8am to 3pm, mid-August to mid-June).</Row>
  <Row label="Likes and filters">Like and reaction counts hidden from teens. Filters that imitate cosmetic surgery banned for them.</Row>
  <Row label="Feed choice">Teens can choose a feed that is not ranked by Meta’s recommendation algorithm.</Row>
  <Row label="Age checks">“Robust” measures to detect users under 18 and to find and remove users under 13.</Row>
  <Row label="Parents">Stronger supervision tools. According to Al Jazeera, parents will see time spent and who their teen is talking to, and get alerts when a teen first messages an adult account or searches for terms linked to suicide, self-harm or eating disorders.</Row>
  <Row label="Oversight">An independent auditor with broad access to Meta’s information, which can take concerns straight to the attorneys general. Meta is also barred from making misleading statements about its safety features.</Row>
</Rows>

Messaging is not covered by the time limit or the night block, according to Meta’s description; teens can still send and receive direct messages. Reports differ slightly on the timetable, but most say the non-algorithmic feed must arrive within about four months of the court’s approval, most other changes within six months (around late February 2027), and the main age-checking requirements within a year.

### The money

<Sidenote term="Attorney general">In the US, each state elects or appoints its own attorney general, the state’s chief lawyer. They can sue companies on behalf of the state’s residents, usually under consumer protection laws, and often team up across party lines.</Sidenote>

The payments are spread over ten years and come in two parts. About $12.7 billion is guaranteed, payable in yearly instalments starting in 2026, according to Meta’s quarterly filing. A further $5 billion or so is contingent: Meta pays it only if Snap, TikTok and YouTube adopt equivalent teen protections. Meta has not yet counted that part as a cost. Of the guaranteed money, California expects between $1.5 billion and $2.1 billion; its share is meant for preventing and treating harm to young people linked to social media, though the state’s legislature decides exactly how it is spent.

For scale: Meta’s revenue in 2025 was about $201 billion, and its profit about $60 billion. The guaranteed payments, spread over a decade, come to roughly 6% of one year’s revenue. When Meta took a $9.9 billion charge for the settlement in October, its share price barely moved. On the day the deal was announced it rose 2.2%.

### What it does not do

The deal leaves a lot alone, and its critics have been quick to point out what. It does not end infinite scroll, autoplay or the personalised recommendation algorithm (teens can opt out of the algorithm, but it stays on by default). It does not restrict targeted advertising. It does not cover disappearing messages, livestreaming or AI chatbots. Ella Bradshaw of the British children’s charity NSPCC called it “piecemeal action” with “important gaps”. It applies only in the United States; Meta has not said whether it will extend the changes elsewhere, and South Korea’s media regulator has already called for them to apply worldwide.

Most importantly, it settles only the states’ claims. More than 10,000 lawsuits by individuals and almost 800 by school districts are still going, and the next personal-injury trial starts in Los Angeles on 28 October. Florida and New Mexico declined to join. In New Mexico, Meta now faces nearly $1 billion in judgments from the child-safety case alone, which it is appealing.

## How the lawsuits got around Big Tech’s legal shields

For most of the internet’s history, a case like this would have been thrown out early. Understanding why it wasn’t is the key to the whole story.

### Section 230

<Sidenote term="Section 230">A 1996 US law saying online services can’t be treated as the “publisher or speaker” of what their users post. It means a platform usually can’t be sued over a harmful post, video or message someone else created.</Sidenote>

**Section 230** of the Communications Decency Act was written in 1996, when the web was young. Its central sentence says that no provider of an online service “shall be treated as the publisher or speaker” of information provided by someone else. Courts read it broadly from the start. In an early case in 1997, a federal appeals court said it created a “federal immunity” against lawsuits that would hold platforms responsible for their users’ content, reasoning that facing lawsuits over millions of posts would push companies to censor heavily or shut down.

That immunity let social media grow up. It also meant that when parents sued a platform because their child had been harmed by something they saw there, the case nearly always failed: the harm came from content, and content was someone else’s responsibility. The US Supreme Court had the chance to narrow the law in 2023, in a case about YouTube recommending terrorist videos, and declined to decide the question.

### The product-design route

The lawyers bringing these cases found a different way in. <mark>Instead of blaming the content, they blamed the design.</mark> Their argument was that apps are products, like cars or toys, and that the companies are responsible for how they built them, whatever users post.

<Chain>

1. **Name the features, not the posts.** The lawsuits focused on things Meta itself built: infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications timed to pull users back, like counts, beauty filters, weak age checks and parental controls that were hard to use.
2. **Call them defects.** Under product-liability law, a maker can be liable if a product is designed in a way that makes it unreasonably dangerous, or if it fails to warn users about risks it knows about. The plaintiffs argued these features were designed to maximise time spent, and that Meta knew they harmed young users.
3. **Show it isn’t about speech.** A notification sent at 2am, or a missing time limit, harms a user regardless of what the content is. So, the argument ran, holding Meta responsible doesn’t treat it as the “publisher” of anyone else’s words.
4. **Add deception.** The states’ case leaned heavily on consumer protection law: they argued Meta publicly claimed its apps were safe while its own research said otherwise. A company’s own statements are its own speech, not a third party’s, so Section 230 does not cover them.

</Chain>

The judges largely accepted the distinction. In November 2023, Judge Gonzalez Rogers dismissed claims that depended on content (for example, that algorithms showed harmful posts) but let design and failure-to-warn claims proceed. In October 2024 she ruled that much of the states’ consumer protection case could go ahead too. In Los Angeles, Judge Kuhl ruled in November 2025 that features such as notification timing, engagement loops and the absence of meaningful parental controls were not protected. A federal appeals court had used similar reasoning in 2021, when it let a lawsuit go ahead against Snap over a “speed filter” that parents said encouraged reckless driving.

<Sidenote term="First Amendment">The part of the US Constitution that protects freedom of speech. Tech companies argue that deciding what to show users, and how, is a form of protected editorial judgment.</Sidenote>

### The free-speech defence

The companies had a second line of defence: the **First Amendment**. They argued that choosing what to show users, in what order, is editorial judgment, like a newspaper deciding its front page, and that the government can’t punish it. The Supreme Court gave that argument support in 2024, when it said platforms’ content moderation is a form of protected expression.

The courts didn’t reject the argument outright. They drew lines around it. Judge Kuhl acknowledged that a jury “might find that Plaintiffs’ harms resulted from the content to which they were exposed”, which would bring the free-speech and Section 230 protections back into play. In New Mexico, the judge who found Meta a public nuisance refused to order changes to its algorithm, citing exactly those conflicts. The result is that design features unconnected to content are open to challenge; the algorithm that chooses what content to show is still mostly protected.

That line is disputed. Eric Goldman, a law professor at Santa Clara University and a prominent defender of Section 230, argues that the difference between design and content is illusory: infinite scroll is only harmful if there is something worth scrolling. Mike Masnick of the tech blog Techdirt called the Los Angeles ruling an example of “bad defendants make bad law”. The question has not been settled by the Supreme Court, and Meta’s appeal of the Los Angeles verdict could eventually take it there.

### Why the timing mattered

One more ruling changed the calculations. Section 230 used to work as an early exit: a company could ask a judge to dismiss a case at the start, and if refused, appeal immediately. On 10 August 2026, the federal appeals court for the western US ruled that Section 230 is a defence to liability, not an immunity from being sued at all, so the companies could not appeal until after a trial. Meta lost its bid to delay the states’ trial the same week. <mark>The old shield had become something you could only raise after a jury had heard the evidence.</mark>

## The verdicts that came first

Two juries had found against Meta before the states’ trial even started.

### The case of K.G.M.

The first case to reach a jury was brought by a young woman identified in court as K.G.M. According to the evidence at trial, she started using YouTube at 6 and Instagram at 9, and had posted 284 YouTube videos by the end of primary school. She testified that she developed anxiety and depression from around the age of 10, later diagnosed along with body dysmorphia, and described buying likes, filtering nearly every photo and checking notifications during class. Snap and TikTok settled with her before trial. Meta’s founder Mark Zuckerberg and Instagram’s head Adam Mosseri both testified.

Meta argued that her problems came from a troubled family life, not its apps, and that teenage mental health “cannot be linked to a single app”. YouTube argued it is a video platform, not social media. On 25 March 2026 the jury disagreed: it found both companies negligent in the design of their apps, found the negligence a substantial factor in her harm, and found they had failed to warn of the risks. It awarded $3 million in compensation and $3 million in punitive damages, split 70% to Meta and 30% to YouTube. Both have appealed.

Six million dollars is nothing to companies of this size. What mattered was the precedent. K.G.M. was a **bellwether**: one of a few cases picked to test how juries react, so that the thousands of similar cases can be valued. A loss in the first bellwether tells every other plaintiff’s lawyer, and every investor, that juries will believe this kind of claim.

<Sidenote term="Bellwether trial">When thousands of similar lawsuits are grouped together, a few representative cases are tried first. Their results help both sides estimate what the rest are worth and often push them towards a wider settlement.</Sidenote>

### New Mexico

The day before, a jury in Santa Fe had found that Meta knowingly harmed children’s mental health and concealed what it knew about child sexual exploitation on its platforms, violating the state’s consumer protection law. It imposed $375 million in penalties, less than a fifth of what the state sought. In August, the judge went further, ruling that Meta’s platforms were a **public nuisance**, a legal theory usually used for things like polluted rivers, and ordering it to pay $567 million into a fund to reduce harm to young people, with court-supervised changes to its apps for five years. A tech policy newsletter called it a first for social media. Meta is appealing both.

### Inside the Oakland trial

The states’ trial opened on 18 August. California’s lawyer, Deputy Attorney General Megan O’Neill, summed up Meta’s business in four words: “hook” young users, “hold” them as long as possible, “harvest” their data, and “hide” the truth. The states built their case on Meta’s own internal documents. According to trial reporting, one internal email to Mosseri said “teen time spent” was the goal, and some employees described Instagram as a “drug” and themselves as “pushers”.

The states’ first witness was Arturo Béjar, a former Meta safety engineer turned whistleblower, whom Meta had tried and failed to keep off the stand. He testified that Meta published statistics on rule-breaking content that “create a false impression of safety”, and that the company used the phrase “problematic use” instead of “addiction”. When Mosseri testified for Meta, he was questioned about Instagram’s “Take a Break” feature: an internal document showed that at one point only 1.8% of teens used it. The reminders “helped, but not nearly as much as we hoped,” he said. The settlement was announced the day after.

## Why did Meta settle?

Meta has not given its reasons beyond saying that teen safety is “an absolute imperative” and that the problem needs “an industry-wide solution”. But the incentives are not hard to read. Put together, they suggest Meta settled not because it expected to lose everything, but because settling let it trade an unknowable risk for a known cost, and turn a defeat into a rule its rivals may have to follow.

<WhyList>
<Why title="The downside had no ceiling">

State consumer protection laws impose penalties per violation, and the states argued that each young user, or each day of use, could count. Meta itself told the court penalties could theoretically reach $1.4 trillion; the states suggested about $200 billion would be appropriate. Neither number was likely, but even a fraction of the second would have dwarfed $18 billion. New Mexico showed what per-violation penalties can do: in a separate privacy case linked to the Cambridge Analytica scandal, a judge there ordered Meta on 21 October to pay $25 billion, after a jury counted more than 43 million violations. Meta is appealing.

</Why>
<Why title="The legal shields had failed, and couldn’t be tested early">

After the Los Angeles and New Mexico verdicts and the August appeals court ruling, Meta could no longer count on Section 230 or the First Amendment to end the case before a jury heard the evidence. Its best chance of winning those arguments was on appeal, years later, after a possible huge judgment. That is a bad bet for any company.

</Why>
<Why title="The evidence was getting worse in public">

Each day of trial put more internal documents into the news. Reports say Zuckerberg had been expected to testify; jurors in Los Angeles had said afterwards that his changing testimony there “didn’t sit well” with them. Settling stopped the flow, and it came with no admission of wrongdoing, which matters because thousands of other plaintiffs would love to quote one.

</Why>
<Why title="The cost was affordable">

The guaranteed payments come to a little over a billion dollars a year for ten years, for a company making about $60 billion a year in profit. Markets treated it as a manageable expense, not a threat to the business. The product changes cost something too, because less teen time on the apps means fewer ads shown to them, but Meta does not publish how much of its revenue comes from under-18s, and teens are a small share of its more than three billion daily users.

</Why>
<Why title="It could turn a loss into an industry rule">

The contingent structure is the most interesting part. Meta pays about $5 billion more only if TikTok, YouTube and Snap adopt the same protections, and if they do, Meta’s limits tighten too. Meta’s chief legal officer, CJ Mahoney, said that because “teens move fluidly across dozens of apps, we need an industry-wide solution”, and called on TikTok and YouTube to adopt the framework “right away”. That is a reasonable point about how teenagers use apps. It is also commercially convenient: a two-hour limit hurts Meta far more if teenagers can simply switch to TikTok for the rest of the evening. None of the rivals has agreed, and nothing obliges them to, but they face the same kinds of lawsuits, and legal experts say the deal puts them on notice.

</Why>
</WhyList>

<Answer title="So did Meta settle because it was guilty?">

The settlement does not tell us. Meta admitted nothing, and settling is often rational even for a company that believes it would eventually win. What it does show is that the legal ground has shifted. Once juries had accepted the design-defect argument and courts had stopped dismissing these cases early, a trial became a gamble with a potentially enormous downside and little upside. Meta chose a large but bounded payment, an end to damaging publicity, and terms that may force its competitors into the same restrictions. Whether its apps actually caused the harms alleged is a question the settlement deliberately leaves open.

</Answer>

## The tobacco and opioid playbook

Commentators were quick to call this social media’s “Big Tobacco moment”. The comparison is useful, both for what it gets right and for where it breaks down.

### What the tobacco deal did

In November 1998, the four biggest US cigarette makers signed the **Master Settlement Agreement** with 46 states, which had sued to recover the cost of treating smoking-related illness through public health insurance. The companies agreed to pay at least $206 billion over the first 25 years, with payments continuing for as long as they sell cigarettes. They also accepted marketing limits: no billboards, no transit advertising, restrictions on sponsorships, and a promise not to target young people. The deal funded an anti-smoking group, now the Truth Initiative, and shut down the industry’s research and lobbying bodies.

Like the Meta case, the tobacco litigation was powered by internal documents showing the companies knew more about the harm than they said. Like Meta, the tobacco companies settled rather than risk a run of state trials. And like the Meta deal, the tobacco settlement combined money with rules about how the product could be sold.

### What went wrong with it

The tobacco deal is also a warning. States were free to spend the money as they liked, and most did not spend much of it on reducing smoking; one critic estimates less than 3% of each dollar went to prevention programmes. Many states borrowed against future payments by issuing “tobacco bonds”, which gave them a financial stake in cigarette sales continuing. Critics argued the deal protected the big incumbents by making it harder for new competitors to undercut them, although a federal court rejected claims that it created an illegal cartel. Smoking still fell sharply in the decades that followed, but mostly because of taxes, smoking bans and changing norms, not the settlement alone.

<Sidenote term="Public nuisance">An old legal idea that lets governments sue over something that harms the public at large, such as pollution. Its use against products like opioids and social media is controversial, because it can make a company liable for broad social problems.</Sidenote>

### The opioid cases

The opioid litigation of the 2010s and 2020s offers a second model. States, cities and counties sued drug makers, distributors and pharmacies over the prescription painkiller epidemic, often using **public nuisance** law, and won settlements worth tens of billions of dollars. The theory had limits: in 2021, Oklahoma’s Supreme Court overturned a $465 million public nuisance judgment against Johnson & Johnson, ruling that the state’s nuisance law did not extend to the marketing and sale of prescription drugs. New Mexico’s ruling against Meta in August used the same kind of theory against a social media company for the first time.

### Where the comparison breaks down

There are three big differences.

- **The science.** The link between smoking and lung cancer is among the strongest findings in medicine. The evidence that social media harms teenagers’ mental health is real but contested; serious researchers disagree about how big the effect is and which way causation runs (see [section 11](#where-reasonable-people-disagree)).
- **Speech.** Cigarettes don’t have free-speech rights. Social media apps carry people’s words, pictures and friendships, which is why the legal fight was so hard and why the settlement leaves the algorithm largely untouched.
- **Benefits.** Teenagers use these apps to talk to friends, find communities and learn. Restricting them has costs that restricting cigarettes did not.

The critics of the comparison go further. Scott Galloway, a marketing professor and long-time critic of Big Tech, points out that the tobacco companies are still selling cigarettes, and argues that the Meta settlement doesn’t change Meta’s underlying incentive to maximise attention. Techdirt’s Masnick argues the opposite worry: that every American teenager has been “automatically enrolled in an untested experiment”, and that the auditor checks whether Meta follows the rules but not whether the rules work.

## What changes for teenagers, rivals and other countries

### For teenagers and parents

Most American teenagers use these apps. In Pew Research Center’s autumn 2025 survey, about six in ten US teens aged 13 to 17 said they used Instagram, and 55% used it every day; 12% said they were on it “almost constantly”. Facebook is much less popular with teens (31% used it at all). TikTok and YouTube are bigger: about one in five teens said they used each almost constantly.

For teens who use Instagram heavily, a two-hour cap and a night lock are a real change, though several gaps are already obvious. Direct messages are exempt. A parent can lift the limits. Teens can move to TikTok, YouTube or Snapchat, which are not bound by the deal. And everything depends on Meta knowing who is under 18. Teens have long lied about their age; the settlement requires “robust” age checks, which in practice means some combination of AI estimation from selfies or behaviour and third-party verification. Australia’s experience shows how hard that is (see below).

### For rival platforms

TikTok, YouTube and Snap face the same kinds of lawsuits in the same courts. YouTube shared the Los Angeles verdict; Snap and TikTok settled that case before trial, and the next Los Angeles trial starts on 28 October. If juries keep siding with plaintiffs, the rivals face pressure to make similar deals, and Meta’s settlement offers a ready-made template. If they sign up, Meta’s limits tighten to one hour, and the rules start to look like an industry standard set by lawsuit.

### For other countries

The settlement applies only in the US, but it lands in the middle of a global wave of action.

<Rows>
  <Row label="European Union">In July, the European Commission said provisionally that Instagram’s and Facebook’s addictive design, including infinite scroll, autoplay, notifications and recommendations, breaches the Digital Services Act, and that Meta’s time-management tools for teens “can easily be ignored”. A final decision could bring fines of up to 6% of worldwide revenue. Meta disagrees.</Row>
  <Row label="Australia">A ban on social media accounts for under-16s took effect in December 2025, with fines of up to A$49.5 million. Enforcement has been difficult: Al Jazeera reports an eSafety study in August finding more than eight in ten young teens and preteens still used the platforms. A class action against Meta was filed in Australia’s Federal Court this month.</Row>
  <Row label="United Kingdom">The government announced in June a ban on social media for under-16s, to take effect next year, and is considering overnight curfews and limits on infinite scroll for under-18s.</Row>
  <Row label="Brazil and Mexico">Brazil has required since March 2026 that accounts of children under 16 be linked to a parent or guardian. Mexico has announced plans to force Meta, TikTok and X to better protect minors.</Row>
</Rows>

The US deal could become a reference point. Regulators elsewhere can now point to a time limit, a night lock and default-off likes that Meta has agreed are technically workable. Equally, Meta can argue that it has already made changes and that other countries should accept the same package rather than impose their own.

## Litigation doing the job of regulation

Step back and the most striking thing about the settlement is who made it. The rules that will shape how millions of American teenagers use social media were not written by Congress or a regulator. They were negotiated by state lawyers and a technology company, under the pressure of a trial, and will be approved by one federal judge.

### Why Congress hasn’t acted

Congress has debated children’s online safety for years. The main bill, the Kids Online Safety Act, would put a legal “duty of care” on platforms to prevent certain harms to minors. In June 2026 the House passed its own version, the KIDS Act, which drops the duty of care and would override conflicting state laws. In August the Senate Commerce Committee advanced a version that keeps the duty of care. Disagreements between the two chambers have kept the legislation from reaching the president.

<Sidenote term="Duty of care">A legal obligation to take reasonable steps to avoid causing foreseeable harm to others. Applied to platforms, it would make them responsible for designing products with young users’ safety in mind.</Sidenote>

The obstacles are familiar. Free-speech and civil liberties groups warn that a vague duty of care would push platforms to remove lawful content, including information about sexuality or mental health that young people seek out. Privacy advocates warn that age verification means collecting identity data from everyone. The tech industry spends heavily on lobbying. Republicans and Democrats agree that children need protecting but disagree on whether federal law should override state laws, and about content moderation in general. Meanwhile dozens of states have passed their own laws on age verification and app design, many of which have been challenged in court on free-speech grounds.

### Why litigation filled the gap

<Chain>

1. **Congress stalls.** Broad support for “protecting kids” doesn’t translate into agreement on a specific law.
2. **States act through the courts.** Attorneys general don’t need a new law to sue; they can use existing consumer protection and privacy laws.
3. **Discovery finds the documents.** Lawsuits force companies to hand over internal research and emails, which legislators can only request.
4. **Juries and judges create risk.** Verdicts like K.G.M.’s make the threat of trial real.
5. **The company negotiates.** Faced with uncertain liability, the company accepts rules it would have lobbied against in Congress.

</Chain>

This is not new. Tobacco marketing, opioid distribution, and car safety before that, were all shaped as much by lawsuits as by statute. Scholars sometimes call it **regulation through litigation**.

It has real strengths. It moves faster than Congress, it does not depend on a few senators, and it can uncover evidence that companies would never volunteer. It also has real weaknesses. The rules are negotiated in private by the parties, not debated in public. They apply to one company, unless others choose to follow. They are shaped by what can be proven in a particular courtroom rather than by what would work best. And the money tends to go to state budgets and lawyers rather than to the people harmed: the tobacco deal’s record on spending is not encouraging.

## What this means for policymakers

### The policy problem

For governments, the core problem is that a widely used product may be harming a minority of its youngest users, in ways that are hard to measure, through features that are also what make it popular. The harms (lost sleep, anxiety, body-image problems, compulsive use) are diffuse and partly cultural. The products are made by a handful of foreign or out-of-state companies that operate globally. And the evidence is not settled enough to say with confidence which interventions will help.

### The challenges and trade-offs

<Rows>
  <Row label="Evidence vs urgency">Waiting for scientific consensus could take years, during which a generation grows up. Acting now risks imposing costly rules that don’t work, or that cause harms of their own. Policymakers have to decide how much precaution is justified by uncertain evidence.</Row>
  <Row label="Who bears the costs">Time limits and bans fall on all teens, including the many who use apps without harm and those, such as isolated or LGBTQ young people, who find support online. Age checks fall on adults too, who must prove their age and hand over data.</Row>
  <Row label="Privacy vs protection">Every age-based rule depends on knowing users’ ages. Reliable checks need identity documents or biometric estimates; privacy-friendly ones are easy to fool. Australia has struggled with exactly this.</Row>
  <Row label="Speech">In the US, the First Amendment limits what governments can require of platforms. Rules aimed at design rather than content are more likely to survive, which is why lawsuits targeted design.</Row>
  <Row label="Displacement">Restricting one app pushes users to another. Rules applied to one company, as in this settlement, are especially leaky; rules applied industry-wide are harder to pass.</Row>
  <Row label="One country’s reach">No single government can regulate a global platform alone. Meta may apply US-only changes, and smaller countries have little leverage. Coordinated action, or a large market like the EU, carries more weight.</Row>
</Rows>

### The tools on the table

- **Lawsuits and settlements.** What the US states have used. Powerful for uncovering evidence and forcing change, but slow, piecemeal and undemocratic in how rules are set.
- **Design codes and a duty of care.** Laws that require platforms to design for children’s safety by default, as the UK’s age-appropriate design code does and the Kids Online Safety Act would. They target the mechanism directly but depend on vague standards that regulators and courts must fill in.
- **Age limits and bans.** Australia’s under-16 ban, and the UK’s planned one. Simple to explain and popular with parents, but hard to enforce and open to the criticism that they cut teens off from benefits as well as harms.
- **Regulator-led risk rules.** The EU’s Digital Services Act requires big platforms to assess and reduce risks, enforced by a regulator that can fine up to 6% of global revenue. More systematic than lawsuits, but slow, and its first big case against Meta’s design is still not final.
- **Age checks at the app-store level.** Proposals, backed by Meta, to make Apple and Google verify ages once when a phone is set up, rather than each app doing it separately. This puts the burden on two other companies, which is partly why Meta favours it.
- **Spending the money well.** If settlement money comes, governments must decide whether it funds youth mental health services, research and prevention, or fills budget gaps. The tobacco deal is the cautionary example.

<Answer title="So what should a government take from this?">

The Meta settlement shows that the companies can build time limits, night locks and stricter defaults when they have to, which weakens the old argument that such rules are unworkable. It also shows the limits of leaving rule-making to the courts: the result covers one company, in one country, with terms shaped by what was risky to argue at trial. For governments, the real choice is not whether to act but how. They can rely on litigation and let the rules emerge case by case. They can legislate design standards that apply to every platform. Or they can try outright age limits and accept the enforcement problems. Each brings costs that someone has to bear. None works well for one country alone.

</Answer>

## Where reasonable people disagree

<Debate topic="On whether social media is really harming teenagers">
  <Side title="The harm is real and large enough to act on">Rates of teenage anxiety and depression in the US and elsewhere rose sharply in the early 2010s, just as smartphones and image-based social media spread. The psychologist Jonathan Haidt, author of The Anxious Generation, argues that the timing, the companies’ own internal research and experiments showing benefits from cutting use add up to a causal story. The US Surgeon General said in 2023 that teens who spend more than three hours a day on social media face double the risk of symptoms of anxiety and depression, and later called for warning labels. On this view, a preponderance of evidence is enough to justify protective rules, as with other products marketed to children, and waiting for perfect proof means failing a generation.</Side>
  <Side title="The evidence is weaker and more mixed than the headlines">Researchers such as Candice Odgers, a psychologist at the University of California, Irvine, argue that most studies find small or inconsistent links, that much of the correlation may run the other way (struggling teens use social media more, rather than social media causing the struggle), and that the book’s claim that technology is “rewiring” children’s brains “is not supported by science”. Mental health has many causes, including economic pressure, academic stress, less freedom to play independently and better diagnosis. On this view, blaming apps risks distracting from proven treatments and imposing restrictions, such as time limits for every teen, that may not help the minority who are actually harmed.</Side>
</Debate>

<Debate topic="On whether lawsuits are the right way to set the rules">
  <Side title="Litigation is working where politics failed">Congress has failed to pass a children’s online safety law for years while the companies lobbied against every proposal. Lawsuits forced out internal documents the public would never otherwise have seen, produced verdicts from ordinary jurors, and delivered concrete changes for millions of teenagers within months. Tobacco and opioids show that litigation can change industries when legislatures are captured or gridlocked. The settlement is enforceable, independently audited, and was agreed by attorneys general of both parties.</Side>
  <Side title="Policy should be made in public, not negotiated in court">Rules that affect how tens of millions of people use a communication tool should be debated and voted on, not agreed behind closed doors between lawyers and a company. Litigation produces patchy results: one company is bound and its rivals are not, and the terms reflect what was risky to argue at trial rather than what research suggests works. The design-versus-content line that made the lawsuits possible is unstable and could chill legitimate features. And settlement money has a poor record of reaching the people who were harmed.</Side>
</Debate>

## Further context: how apps learned to hold attention

Social media companies make most of their money from advertising, and advertising revenue grows with the amount of time and attention users give. That simple fact shaped two decades of design. Facebook’s News Feed (2006) turned a directory of profiles into an endless stream. The “like” button (2009) gave users a quick, measurable hit of social approval. Infinite scroll removed the natural stopping point at the bottom of a page; its inventor, Aza Raskin, has since said he regrets how it has been used. Push notifications pulled users back throughout the day. Recommendation algorithms learned what kept each person watching.

Facebook bought Instagram in 2012 for about $1 billion, when it had 13 employees. Instagram’s focus on photos, filters and follower counts made it especially central to teenage social life, and especially tied to appearance. By the late 2010s, Meta’s own researchers were studying its effects on young users. The “Facebook Files” in 2021 made some of that research public, and Meta paused plans for an Instagram for children under 13. Two years later, the state attorneys general sued, citing a 2021 internal document that described young users as “a valuable but untapped audience”.

The companies argue that they have added many safety tools over the years: Instagram launched “teen accounts” with stricter default settings in 2024, and the apps offered time reminders and parental controls before any court required them. The states’ argument, and the evidence from the Oakland trial, is that those tools were optional, little used and designed not to dent engagement. The settlement’s main change is to turn some of them from opt-in to default.

## Key terms

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Section 230">The 1996 US law that stops online services being treated as the publisher of what their users post. The main reason platforms were rarely sued over user content.</Term>
  <Term name="Product liability">The area of law that holds makers responsible for harm caused by defective products, including a dangerous design or a failure to warn.</Term>
  <Term name="Design defect">A flaw in how a product is designed, rather than made, that makes it unreasonably dangerous. The core claim in the social media cases.</Term>
  <Term name="Failure to warn">Liability for not telling users about risks a maker knew or should have known about.</Term>
  <Term name="Bellwether trial">An early test trial picked from a large group of similar cases, used to gauge how juries will react.</Term>
  <Term name="Multidistrict litigation (MDL)">A US federal procedure that sends similar cases from around the country to one judge for pre-trial handling.</Term>
  <Term name="Consent judgment">A settlement approved and entered by a court, which makes its terms enforceable as a court order.</Term>
  <Term name="Public nuisance">A legal theory allowing governments to sue over harm to the public at large; used against opioid makers and now, in New Mexico, against Meta.</Term>
  <Term name="Duty of care">A legal obligation to take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable harm. The central and most disputed part of the Kids Online Safety Act.</Term>
  <Term name="Age assurance">Methods of estimating or verifying a user’s age, from asking for a birth date to ID checks and AI analysis of selfies.</Term>
</KeyTerms>

### Concepts worth learning properly

<Concepts>
  <Concept title="Externalities">Costs a business imposes on others without paying for them. The states’ case is that teen mental health costs fell on families, schools and public health systems, not on Meta.</Concept>
  <Concept title="The attention economy">When a product is free and paid for by advertising, the business is selling users’ attention, which gives it an incentive to capture as much as possible.</Concept>
  <Concept title="Collective action and first-mover problems">Why no single platform wants to limit teen use alone, since users would switch to rivals, and why Meta’s deal tries to pull competitors in.</Concept>
  <Concept title="The precautionary principle">The idea that when an activity may cause serious harm, protective action can be justified before the science is settled. Central to the debate over how strong the evidence must be.</Concept>
</Concepts>
