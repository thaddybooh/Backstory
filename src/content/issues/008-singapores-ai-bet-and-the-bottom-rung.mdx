<Overview>

Singapore has made artificial intelligence the centre of its economic plan. In February’s Budget, Prime Minister Lawrence Wong set up a National AI Council that he chairs himself, launched national “AI missions” in manufacturing, logistics, finance and healthcare, and gave companies tax breaks and grants to adopt AI. Two weeks later, answering MPs worried about what this would do to jobs, he made a promise: “We will not have jobless growth in Singapore.” AI would be used to grow the economy, he said, and that growth would turn into good jobs and better wages.

Eight months on, the promise is being tested. The headline numbers still look strong: unemployment was 1.9% in June, and employment has grown for 19 quarters in a row. But underneath, there are signs of strain. Retrenchments reached 4,620 in April–June, the most since the pandemic year of 2020, concentrated in manufacturing, information and communications, and finance. Fewer than 55% of retrenched residents found a new job within six months, down from 61% the quarter before. Only 74.4% of last year’s university graduates were in full-time work six months after their exams, down from 79.4%. In one survey, 58% of workers said they feared AI could take their job within two years.

Then, on 12 October, both of Singapore’s top leaders raised the same alarm on the same day. Mr Wong told a banking conference the government was concerned that “that bottom rung of the career ladder” could be broken by AI, because the routine work that trains young staff is exactly what AI does best. President Tharman Shanmugaratnam told the IMF and World Bank meetings in Bangkok that the first job after graduation “is now seeing less demand”. This issue explains what Singapore is betting on, what the job numbers do and don’t show, why people are uneasy when unemployment is so low, and what it would take to adopt AI without hollowing out the jobs where careers begin.

</Overview>

## What Singapore is betting on

<Sidenote term="Generative AI">AI systems that produce text, images, code or other content in response to instructions, such as ChatGPT. They are good at many routine office tasks, such as drafting, summarising and analysing documents.</Sidenote>

Singapore’s government has backed AI for most of a decade, but 2026 is the year it went, in the phrase used across Singapore’s media, “all in”. The reasons are structural. Singapore has no natural resources, a small and ageing workforce, and an economy built on being a more productive, more trusted place to do business than its neighbours. Its leaders see AI as a way to keep that edge, and fear that if Singapore adopts it more slowly than competitors, investment and jobs will go elsewhere.

### The plan

The Budget announced on 12 February, worth S$154.7 billion in total, set out the push:

<Rows>
  <Row label="Leadership">A **National AI Council**, chaired by the prime minister with six other ministers, to set direction, commission projects and remove regulatory obstacles.</Row>
  <Row label="AI missions">National missions to use AI in four sectors: advanced manufacturing, connectivity and logistics, finance, and healthcare.</Row>
  <Row label="Business">Companies can deduct 400% of qualifying AI spending from their taxes under the Enterprise Innovation Scheme, up to S$50,000 a year, for 2027 and 2028. Grants for digital tools now cover more AI products, and a **Champions of AI** programme gives tailored help to firms that want to reorganise around AI.</Row>
  <Row label="Workers">A redesigned SkillsFuture website to make AI courses easier to find, six months of free premium AI tools for people who take selected courses, more AI training in universities and polytechnics, and a merger of SkillsFuture Singapore and Workforce Singapore into one agency for training, career guidance and job matching.</Row>
  <Row label="Research">S$37 billion for the national research plan to 2030. A separate S$1 billion for AI research to 2030 was announced in January.</Row>
</Rows>

The Economic Strategy Review, a two-year study of how Singapore should grow, came to a similar conclusion. Its final recommendations, released in May, argue that Singapore should not try to compete with much bigger economies in building the most advanced AI models or the largest data centres. Instead it should be the place where AI is developed, tested, deployed and scaled: a nation of skilled AI users rather than a nation of AI builders.

### Why business is listening

Singapore’s companies are unusually keen. In a PwC survey of executives at large firms, published in April, 67% of the Singapore respondents said their appetite for AI investment had risen, against 41% worldwide, and 17% said they were already using AI in its more autonomous forms, more than double the global rate. Most of the Singapore sample were banks and technology companies, the sectors that dominate the city’s office jobs.

### The promise

The government knows the risk. When MPs raised fears during the Budget debate that heavy AI use would cut training, make it harder for older workers to get back into work and hollow out entry-level jobs, Mr Wong replied on 26 February that “these concerns are real”, and promised: “We will not have jobless growth in Singapore.” He said the evidence “does not point to widespread displacement”, citing a record share of permanent employees of nearly 91% and the fact that more than 40% of vacancies were entry-level professional jobs. He also endorsed three requests from the labour movement, NTUC: make every worker ready for AI, strengthen support and safeguards, and better protect professionals, managers, executives and technicians (PMETs).

## What the job numbers actually show

The government and its critics can both point to the numbers, because the numbers say different things depending on which you look at.

### The case that things are fine

- **Unemployment is low.** Overall unemployment was 1.9% in June, the resident rate 2.9% and the citizen rate 3.0%. The Ministry of Manpower calls these “low and stable”.
- **Jobs are still growing.** Total employment rose by 11,400 in April–June, the 19th quarterly rise in a row.
- **There are still more jobs than jobseekers.** There were 1.48 vacancies for every unemployed person in June.
- **Entry-level jobs are still there.** 31,700 vacancies, 45.3% of the total, were entry-level PMET jobs. The government has repeatedly pointed to this.
- **Most retrenched professionals recover.** About 70% of retrenched PMETs find work within a year and about 80% within two years. Of those who are re-employed, about six in ten earn more than before.
- **Firms say they are redesigning jobs, not cutting them.** Ms Lau told Parliament that the ministry’s surveys found “only a small minority” of firms adopting AI reduced their headcount, while more created new roles or redesigned jobs.

### The case that something is changing

- **Retrenchments are at a five-year high.** 4,620 people were laid off in April–June, 2.0 for every 1,000 employees, up from 1.6 in January–March. Almost three in four cases were blamed on restructuring rather than falling business, and the rise came mainly from manufacturing, information and communications, and financial services.
- **It is taking longer to recover.** The share of retrenched residents back in work within six months fell from 60.7% to 54.9% in a single quarter. Of those re-employed on lower pay, the median pay cut was about 25%.
- **Graduates are hit.** Retrenched residents are increasingly educated: 66.4% had a degree in 2025, up from 51.0% in 2021. Professional jobs are now more likely to be cut than others: 3.2 per 1,000 PMET employees were retrenched in April–June.
- **Professional vacancies are falling.** Vacancies dropped from 73,300 in March to 68,600 in June, mainly fewer PMET openings in finance and information and communications, exactly the sectors where AI adoption is fastest.
- **New graduates are struggling more.** Of university graduates from 2025, 74.4% had full-time jobs six months after their exams, down from 79.4% for the year before. The share who applied but got no offers doubled in two years, from 4.1% to 8.5%. The universities said hiring for information and communications was “more subdued than in prior years”.
- **Locals are getting a smaller share of new jobs.** Of the 11,400 new jobs in April–June, only 2,200 went to residents; 9,200 went to foreign workers, mostly work permit holders in construction and manufacturing.

<mark>The overall labour market is still tight, but the strain is concentrated on exactly the people Singapore told to study hard and aim for office careers: graduates and professionals in finance and tech.</mark>

### Is any of it AI?

No one knows, and that is part of the problem. When the Workers’ Party MP He Ting Ru asked in Parliament on 6 October how many retrenchments were linked to AI, the ministry said it is unclear whether AI is driving them, because its effect is hard to separate from wider business changes. It said it would “refine our surveys and market-sensing mechanisms”, but did not commit to counting AI-related retrenchments separately.

There are other plausible explanations. Graduate hiring was unusually strong after the pandemic and was always likely to cool. Trade is under pressure: the United States imposed a 12.5% tariff on Singapore in July, and energy prices have been high since the war with Iran began in February. Several firms that cut jobs, including Singapore’s three biggest banks, which shed nearly 3,000 roles in 2025, gave reasons such as merging acquired businesses and not renewing contracts. But some have pointed directly to AI: DBS when it announced its 4,000 contract job cuts, and Shopee when it cut jobs, including software engineers, in June.

## The bottom rung problem

What alarmed Mr Wong in October was a specific mechanism, not job losses in general. Speaking at a DBS conference on 12 October, he said: “We are concerned that there may be that bottom rung of the career ladder that is broken, and young people may not get sufficient training, and we want companies to pay attention to this.” He gave examples of junior tasks AI can now do: taking notes, drafting speeches, preparing presentations for bosses.

<Chain>

1. **Junior staff learn by doing routine work.** In banking, law, consulting, accounting or software, new hires spend their first years on research, drafting, checking and summarising. It is often dull, but it is how they learn the job.
2. **That routine work is what AI does best.** Generative AI can draft a memo, summarise a contract or write simple code in seconds.
3. **Firms need fewer juniors.** If AI handles the routine work, a team needs fewer new graduates. Senior staff, whose judgement and client relationships AI cannot yet replace, are kept.
4. **Fewer juniors become seniors.** Firms that stop hiring juniors today will have fewer experienced professionals in ten years, because the training ground has gone.
5. **Entry requirements rise.** Firms increasingly want new hires who already have experience, which new graduates cannot get without a first job. Malaysian employers’ representatives describe it as “no experience, no job; no job, no experience”.

</Chain>

Mr Wong said firms must be careful not to “automate away” the learning that comes from routine work, and that the government was “looking at whether there is a need for new programmes, new schemes”. He called Singapore’s approach of retraining every worker the “harder road”, but more sustainable than replacing workers with AI for short-term gains, and warned that leaving workers behind would provoke “an even stronger backlash to AI and technology”. He was also careful: “We are not saying zero retrenchments.”

On the same day, President Tharman made the same point to finance ministers and central bankers at the IMF and World Bank meetings in Bangkok. “The first rung in employment, your first job upon graduation, is now seeing less demand,” he said. “The evidence is already very clear, it’s clearest in the most AI-exposed sectors.” He urged governments to give firms incentives to hire fresh graduates, according to reports of his speech, and warned of a “new divergence” between rich and developing countries.

### What the evidence says

The best-known evidence comes from the United States. Economists at Stanford University, led by Erik Brynjolfsson, used payroll data covering millions of American workers to show that employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI, such as software development and customer service, fell by about 13% relative to other workers after generative AI spread, in a 2025 version of their study; a revised version in August 2026 puts the gap higher. The decline was concentrated in jobs where AI automates tasks rather than helps workers do them. The authors call their findings early warning signs, “canaries in the coal mine”, rather than proof, and found no sign of economy-wide job losses.

Other economists are more sceptical. A 2025 study of Danish workers by Anders Humlum and Emilie Vestergaard found no effect of workplace AI use on earnings or hours worked. Mikhail Rosli of Malaysia’s Khazanah Research Institute notes that the shift towards demanding more experience from new hires began around 2019, before ChatGPT, and argues that “trends that start before the supposed cause cannot be attributed to said cause”. Interest rates, the end of pandemic-era hiring and remote work may matter as much as AI.

## Why are people uneasy when unemployment is so low?

Singapore’s unemployment rate would be the envy of most countries. Yet worry about AI is widespread: 58% of workers in ManpowerGroup’s survey feared AI could take their job within two years, and 39% thought they could lose their job within six months. Other surveys find much lower figures, from about 11% to 30% depending on how the question is asked. The gap between the statistics and the mood has several causes.

<WhyList>
<Why title="The averages hide who is hurting">

The 1.9% figure includes foreign workers on work passes, who generally must leave Singapore if they lose their jobs and so rarely show up as unemployed. The citizen rate is 3.0%. And the strain is concentrated: retrenchments are rising among graduates and professionals in finance and tech, re-employment is slowing, and new graduates are finding it harder to get full-time work. For a 23-year-old who has applied for dozens of jobs without an offer, the national rate is not much comfort.

</Why>
<Why title="It is hitting the people who did everything right">

For decades, Singapore’s bargain was that studying hard and getting a degree led to a secure professional career. AI threatens precisely the kind of routine office work that graduates start in. When two-thirds of retrenched residents have degrees, the promise that education guarantees security looks weaker, and the anxiety spreads to parents and students choosing courses.

</Why>
<Why title="The safety net is thin by design">

Singapore has no unemployment insurance. The government has long argued that generous benefits would discourage work, preferring to spend on training and wage subsidies. The SkillsFuture Jobseeker Support Scheme, introduced in 2025, pays up to S$6,000 over six months to people who earned S$5,000 a month or less. For a professional with a mortgage and family, losing a job is a bigger financial shock than in countries with more generous insurance, and that raises the stakes of every rumour about AI.

</Why>
<Why title="People can’t tell if AI is to blame">

Firms rarely say they are replacing staff with AI, and the government does not count AI-related retrenchments separately. When workers see vacancies fall in the most AI-exposed sectors while officials say there is no evidence of widespread displacement, both can be true, but it leaves people to fill the gap with headlines from abroad and stories from friends.

</Why>
<Why title="The government is both cheerleader and referee">

The same government that is pushing companies to adopt AI as fast as possible, with tax breaks and grants, is also responsible for protecting the workers who might lose out. Its promise of no jobless growth is a political commitment, not a law or a measurable target. Some workers suspect that when the two goals clash, growth will win.

</Why>
</WhyList>

<Answer title="So is the government’s bet right?">

The bet itself is hard to argue with. A small, open, high-cost economy cannot opt out of a technology its competitors are adopting; if Singapore’s banks, logistics firms and factories don’t use AI, those jobs are more likely to move elsewhere than to stay unchanged. Choosing to be a skilled user of AI, rather than trying to build frontier models, plays to Singapore’s strengths.

The harder question is whether “no jobless growth” is the right test. Overall employment may keep growing while a particular group, new graduates and mid-career professionals in office jobs, bears most of the cost. The evidence so far is early and mixed: there are real warning signs, and no proof that AI is causing them. What would make the bet work is not slowing adoption but protecting the bottom rung: making sure firms still hire and train young people, measuring what AI is actually doing to jobs, and giving those who lose out a stronger cushion. On those, the government has so far offered concern and reviews, more than new policy.

</Answer>

## Singapore’s playbook for AI and jobs

Singapore has dealt with waves of job disruption before, from the 1985 recession to the loss of manufacturing jobs to cheaper neighbours. Its approach has been consistent: keep the economy open, let firms restructure, and spend on retraining and wage support rather than unemployment benefits, with unions, employers and government (the “tripartite” partners) negotiating the details. The AI question tests whether that playbook still works.

### What is already in place

- **Training.** SkillsFuture credits for every adult, career conversion programmes that pay part of a worker’s salary while they retrain for a new industry, and enhanced support for workers aged 40 and above. About nine in ten participants in career conversion programmes are still employed two years after starting, the ministry says.
- **Support while looking for work.** The Jobseeker Support Scheme, and a taskforce of the ministry, NTUC and others that contacts workers after a retrenchment to offer coaching and job matching.
- **Rules for employers.** Firms with ten or more staff must notify the ministry of retrenchments. Retrenchment benefits are guided by a tripartite advisory rather than required by law for most workers; about 88% of employers paid them in 2025. Firms receiving government grants are expected to follow the advisory, and the ministry says it will investigate unfair displacement.
- **Foreign worker rules.** The minimum salary a firm must pay local staff before it can hire foreign workers was raised to S$1,800 in the Budget, and the qualifying salaries for Employment Passes and S Passes were raised.
- **Graduate traineeships.** Government-supported traineeships for new graduates who haven’t found jobs, launched in October 2025.

### The options on the table

<Rows>
  <Row label="Pay firms to hire graduates">President Tharman urged governments to give firms incentives to hire fresh graduates, and the Workers’ Party proposes temporary wage subsidies for firms that take on new graduates as apprentices. This directly targets the bottom rung. The risk is paying firms for hires they would have made anyway, and keeping alive roles that AI will make obsolete.</Row>
  <Row label="Redundancy insurance">The Workers’ Party wants a scheme, funded by small contributions from employers and workers (0.1% of salary in total), that would pay retrenched workers 40% of their last salary, up to a cap, for up to six months, at every income level. It would cushion professionals above the Jobseeker scheme’s income limit. The government has preferred a taxpayer-funded scheme with a lower income ceiling, arguing it keeps the focus on getting back to work.</Row>
  <Row label="Stronger retrenchment rules">The Employment Act is under review, including whether retrenchment benefits should be compulsory and whether firms should have to notify the ministry earlier. Stronger rules protect workers but make Singapore slightly less attractive to employers who value flexibility.</Row>
  <Row label="Measure what AI is doing">The ministry could track retrenchments and hiring cuts linked to AI. Better data would let policy respond to real effects rather than fears, but firms may be reluctant to admit AI is the reason.</Row>
  <Row label="Nudge firms to keep training">Mr Wong has asked employers not to “automate away” junior learning and hinted at new schemes. Options include tying AI adoption grants to commitments to hire and train young staff, or funding structured apprenticeships. Conditions on grants could slow AI adoption, which is the opposite of what the Budget aims for.</Row>
  <Row label="Change what universities teach">President Tharman and others want more practical training, internships and workplace attachments at university, so graduates arrive with some of the experience AI has made harder to get on the job.</Row>
</Rows>

### The trade-offs

Every option involves a trade-off between speed and protection. Singapore’s competitiveness depends on firms adopting AI quickly, and anything that makes it costlier to restructure, from mandatory benefits to conditions on grants, slows that down. But if workers conclude that AI benefits companies and shareholders while they bear the risk, the backlash Mr Wong warned about could make adoption harder, not easier. There is also a fiscal question: wage subsidies and insurance cost money at a time when the government is already spending heavily on an ageing population.

<Answer title="So what would it take not to hollow out entry-level jobs?">

Three things, according to the arguments made so far. First, someone has to pay for training young people when AI makes their work less immediately valuable: firms, through apprenticeships and redesigned junior roles, or the government, through hiring subsidies. Second, the government needs to know what is happening: counting AI-related job losses and tracking graduate hiring closely, so that policy responds early, as Mr Wong promised. Third, a stronger cushion for those who lose out, whether a bigger jobseeker scheme or insurance, would make the AI push easier to accept. None of these is free, and each slows adoption a little. That is the price of keeping the promise of no jobless growth meaningful for the people most at risk.

</Answer>

## Where reasonable people disagree

<Debate topic="On whether going all in on AI is the right bet for Singapore">
  <Side title="Singapore has no choice but to lead">A small, open economy that lives by being more productive than its neighbours cannot afford to adopt the most important technology of the age slowly. If Singapore’s banks, ports and factories don’t use AI, the work will move to places that do. Fast adoption also attracts investment and creates new jobs, from AI engineering to roles nobody has invented yet. Past technologies, from containers to the internet, destroyed some jobs and created many more, and Singapore came out ahead each time by embracing change and retraining workers. Unemployment is still under 2%, and the evidence of AI-driven job losses is thin. Slowing down now would trade a speculative risk for a certain loss of competitiveness.</Side>
  <Side title="The speed is the problem">AI is different from past technologies because it targets the cognitive, office work that Singapore’s middle class depends on, and it is spreading faster than any previous technology. Pushing firms to adopt it with tax breaks, while the government admits it cannot measure the effect on jobs, puts the risk on workers and the gains with companies. The early signs, from graduate hiring to retrenchments in finance and tech, are already there. A more cautious approach would tie support for AI adoption to commitments on hiring and training, build a stronger safety net first, and treat “no jobless growth” as a measurable target, not a slogan.</Side>
</Debate>

<Debate topic="On whether Singapore needs unemployment or redundancy insurance">
  <Side title="Yes: the safety net was built for a different era">Singapore’s reliance on training and short-term support made sense when job losses were cyclical and most workers could quickly find similar work. AI may cause longer, structural displacement among professionals with mortgages and families, above the income limit of the current jobseeker scheme. Insurance funded by small contributions would act as an automatic stabiliser in a downturn, let workers take time to find the right job rather than the first one, and, as the Workers’ Party argues, give people the security to take the risks an AI economy requires.</Side>
  <Side title="No: it would weaken what works">Singapore’s low unemployment rests partly on strong incentives to get back to work quickly. Unemployment benefits in other countries have been linked to longer jobless spells. The current approach, combining temporary payments with intensive job matching and paid retraining, keeps the focus on employment, and most retrenched professionals do find work within a year, often at higher pay. Compulsory contributions would also add to employers’ costs. Better-targeted help for the groups hit hardest, such as graduates and older PMETs, would do more good than a universal scheme.</Side>
</Debate>

## Further context: Singapore’s history of disruption

Singapore has reinvented its economy several times since independence in 1965: from entrepôt trade to labour-intensive manufacturing in the 1960s and 70s, to higher-value manufacturing after a deliberate push to raise wages in 1979, to services, finance and research from the 1990s. Each shift destroyed some jobs, and each time the government’s answer was training and moving workers to new industries rather than protecting old ones.

The 1985 recession, when Singapore’s economy shrank for the first time since independence, led to cuts in employers’ CPF contributions to reduce costs. During the 2001 and 2008–09 downturns, the government subsidised wages to keep people in work. SkillsFuture, launched in 2015, gave every adult credits for training. During the pandemic, the government spent heavily on wage support and traineeships. The common thread is tripartism: the government, NTUC and employers negotiating together, which has kept labour relations calm but also means workers rely heavily on the government and unions acting for them.

## Key terms

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Jobless growth">Economic growth that does not create more jobs, or creates them for fewer people. Mr Wong promised Singapore would avoid it.</Term>
  <Term name="Retrenchment">Being laid off because a job is no longer needed, rather than for poor performance. Singapore’s term for redundancy.</Term>
  <Term name="PMET">Professionals, managers, executives and technicians: Singapore’s term for white-collar and skilled workers.</Term>
  <Term name="Re-entry rate">The share of retrenched workers who find a new job within a set time, such as six months.</Term>
  <Term name="Tripartism">The partnership of government, unions (NTUC) and employers that negotiates labour policy in Singapore.</Term>
  <Term name="TAMEM">The Tripartite Advisory on Managing Excess Manpower and Responsible Retrenchment: guidelines on how firms should handle retrenchments, including benefits.</Term>
  <Term name="SkillsFuture">Singapore’s national training programme, including credits every adult can spend on courses.</Term>
  <Term name="Redundancy insurance">A scheme, funded by contributions, that pays workers part of their salary for a time after they are laid off.</Term>
  <Term name="Automation vs augmentation">Whether AI replaces the tasks a worker does (automation) or helps them do more (augmentation). The evidence suggests young workers are hurt mainly by automation.</Term>
</KeyTerms>

### Concepts worth learning properly

<Concepts>
  <Concept title="Skill-biased technological change">The idea that new technology raises demand for some skills and lowers it for others. AI may be the first technology to hit highly educated office workers hardest.</Concept>
  <Concept title="On-the-job training and human capital">How workers become skilled by doing the job. Central to why losing junior roles could harm the economy in the long run.</Concept>
  <Concept title="Moral hazard in unemployment insurance">The concern that benefits reduce the incentive to find work quickly. The core of the debate over Singapore’s safety net.</Concept>
  <Concept title="Lump of labour fallacy">The mistaken belief that there is a fixed amount of work to go round. Useful for thinking about whether AI will destroy jobs overall or shift them.</Concept>
</Concepts>
