<Overview>

On 15 October 2026, MSCI, the company whose stock indexes guide more than $16 trillion of the world’s investments, said Indonesia should no longer be classed as an emerging market. It did not propose the usual step down, to “frontier” market. It proposed something more severe: moving Indonesia to “standalone” status, outside its main global index families altogether, alongside markets such as Lebanon, Nigeria and Jamaica. The reason, MSCI said, was not the economy. It was that international investors could no longer trust the market itself: they could not tell who really owned many listed companies, and they suspected that some share prices were being moved by coordinated trading.

The next day the Jakarta Composite Index fell 11.6%, its worst day since the Asian financial crisis in January 1998, and trading was halted for 30 minutes. It fell more than 5% again on the Monday. By 30 October the index was down about 18% for the month and about 41% for the year, from a record high of 9,134 in January. Foreign investors had pulled about $5 billion out of Indonesian shares this year, and the value of their holdings had nearly halved, from about $250 billion to $130 billion, according to Goldman Sachs. The rupiah has been near record lows against the dollar since June.

<mark>This is not a sudden crisis but the end of a nine-month warning.</mark> MSCI first froze Indonesian stocks in its indexes in January, and the government has spent the year announcing reforms while also unsettling investors in other ways: a costly free meals programme, a central bank pushed to support growth, and an oil shock that hit Indonesia’s trade balance. This issue explains why one index company’s opinion can move a whole country’s market, what is really wrong with Jakarta’s market, and what Indonesia can still do before MSCI decides in June 2027.

</Overview>

## Why does one index company matter so much?

<Sidenote term="Passive fund">An investment fund that does not pick shares but copies an index, buying every stock in it in proportion to its weight. Index funds and most exchange-traded funds (ETFs) work this way.</Sidenote>

Most people who own shares in Indonesia from abroad do not choose Indonesia. They choose an index, and the index chooses Indonesia for them.

MSCI, originally Morgan Stanley Capital International, builds the indexes that much of the world’s investment industry measures itself against. More than $16.5 trillion of funds were tracked against or benchmarked to MSCI indexes in 2025, according to the company. Its most important decision is which family a country belongs to. MSCI sorts markets into four groups:

<Rows>
  <Row label="Developed markets">Rich, open markets such as the US, Japan and most of Europe.</Row>
  <Row label="Emerging markets">Large markets that are open enough for global investors but not fully at developed standards: China, India, Brazil, Korea and, since 1989, Indonesia. A huge pool of money tracks this index.</Row>
  <Row label="Frontier markets">Smaller or harder-to-access markets such as Vietnam, Pakistan and Bangladesh. Far less money follows them.</Row>
  <Row label="Standalone markets">Markets that fall short even of frontier standards, usually because investors cannot get money in or out, or cannot rely on the market. They sit outside MSCI’s main global index families. Today they include Argentina, Lebanon, Nigeria, Jamaica and Panama.</Row>
</Rows>

MSCI judges countries mainly on accessibility, not on how rich they are. Every June it scores each market on five broad areas: how open it is to foreign ownership, how easily money flows in and out, how well its trading and settlement work, what investment products are available, and how stable its rules are. Indonesia’s economy, the biggest in Southeast Asia, is not in question. What is in question is whether foreigners can trust what they are buying.

<mark>The power comes from automatic money.</mark> When a country leaves an index, every passive fund that tracks that index must sell its shares there, whatever the fund’s managers think of the companies. Achmad Sukarsono of the consultancy Control Risks put it simply: if MSCI confirms a downgrade, “index funds would sell Indonesian holdings automatically.” Active fund managers who are judged against the index often follow, because holding a stock outside your benchmark is a bet your bosses must approve.

Estimates of the forced selling differ, because nobody knows exactly how much money tracks each index:

- Mirae Asset Sekuritas estimates about $3 billion, based on Indonesia’s remaining weight of about 0.4% of the MSCI emerging markets index. Citigroup says mid-single-digit billions.
- Goldman Sachs estimates about $5.6 billion if Indonesia is removed from the emerging markets indexes entirely, equal to 18 days of normal trading in those stocks, plus $2.7 billion more if FTSE Russell, the second big index provider, follows MSCI. Active funds benchmarked to the two providers hold more than $8 billion of Indonesian shares on top of that.
- Josua Pardede, chief economist at Bank Permata, puts forced passive selling at $3 billion to $5 billion, with another $15 billion of active money likely to be reconsidered.

Those sums look small next to a market worth around $1 trillion. But they would hit a market that is already thin: Pandu Sjahrir, chief investment officer of the state fund Danantara, said in January that Jakarta trades about $1 billion of shares a day and needs eight to ten times that. And the money is only half of the damage. As Josh Kurlantzick of the Council on Foreign Relations told Fortune, a downgrade is “a loud signal to everyone else that something is wrong.”

### Why standalone, not frontier?

The usual punishment for a struggling emerging market is a move to frontier status, as happened to Pakistan in 2021. MSCI considered that in June and threatened it again in October. It then chose the harsher option because, it said, feedback from investors suggested a frontier reclassification “would not properly recognise the depth of the market problems.” Euronews noted that the proposal would skip frontier status entirely. MSCI says it “expects to proceed with the proposed reclassification unless the consultation identifies clear evidence of material improvements in market governance and functioning.” It will announce its decision by the end of June 2027. Until then, Indonesian stocks stay frozen in its indexes: no additions, no increases in weight.

## What does MSCI say is wrong with Jakarta’s market?

<Sidenote term="Free float">The share of a company’s stock that ordinary investors can actually buy and sell, as opposed to shares held by founders, families, governments or other controlling owners. Index providers weight stocks by their free float, not their total size.</Sidenote>

MSCI’s complaint comes down to two words in its June review: “opacity” and “coordination”. It said that “ongoing opacity in shareholding structures and indications of coordinated trading behavior that undermines proper price formation” were limiting international investors’ “ability to assess true free float and to rely on observed market prices for portfolio construction and index replication.” In plain terms: investors cannot tell how many shares of a company are really available to the public, and they cannot be sure the price on the screen is a real market price.

These two problems feed each other.

**Very few shares are really for sale.** Many of Indonesia’s biggest listed companies are controlled by a founder or family that owns the large majority of the shares. Until this year, the exchange required only 7.5% of a company’s shares to be in public hands.

**Nobody could see who held the rest.** Until this year, investors only had to disclose holdings of 5% or more. A company could appear to have 10% or 15% of its shares in public hands, while in fact some of those were held by investors close to the controlling owner, each holding just under the threshold. Regulators have since cut the disclosure threshold to 1%, and MSCI now uses shareholding data from Indonesia’s central securities depository, which records every holding above 1%.

**A thin float makes a price easy to move.** If only a small slice of a company trades, it takes relatively little buying to push the price up sharply. Indonesians have a name for such shares: *saham gorengan*, “fried stocks”, shares that a group of traders heats up and then sells. Hashim Djojohadikusumo, the president’s brother and a special envoy, told tycoons at a business forum in late October to “stop deep frying stocks immediately because the government intends to take firm action.”

### How a thinly traded stock can game an index

Analysts and the Indonesian press have described how these features can combine into what some call the “MSCI trade”. This is a description of the risk MSCI is pointing to, not a finding about any particular company:

<Chain>

1. **A company lists with very few shares in public hands.** The controlling owner keeps most of them.
2. **The price rises far faster than the business.** With little stock available, steady buying can push the price, and so the company’s market value, to very high levels.
3. **The company becomes big enough for the index.** Index providers include companies by their size. If some of the “public” shares are actually held by friends of the owner, the free float looks bigger than it is, and the company qualifies sooner and with a larger weight.
4. **Index funds must buy.** Once a stock enters an index, passive funds around the world buy it at whatever price the market shows.
5. **Insiders can sell into that demand.** The automatic buyers give controlling owners and their allies a buyer for shares at inflated prices.
6. **Later buyers carry the risk.** When the price is not supported by the business, ordinary investors and the funds that tracked the index are left holding it as it falls.

</Chain>

This is exactly what MSCI means when it says it cannot “rely on observed market prices” for “index replication”. It is not saying every stock is manipulated. It is saying its rules depend on two numbers, the price and the free float, and in Indonesia it can no longer trust either.

### Who has been named?

<Sidenote term="High shareholding concentration (HSC)">A label the Indonesia Stock Exchange gives to stocks where a small number of holders own almost all the shares. MSCI follows a precedent set in Hong Kong in removing such stocks from its indexes.</Sidenote>

In May, MSCI deleted six companies from its main Indonesia index and added none. Two of them, Barito Renewables Energy and Dian Swastatika Sentosa, had been named by the exchange as stocks with high shareholding concentration. Three others, Amman Mineral Internasional, Petrindo Jaya Kreasi and Chandra Asri Pacific, did not make it into MSCI’s small companies index either, because their free float was below 15%. The sixth, the convenience store chain Sumber Alfaria Trijaya, was moved down to the small companies index. Bloomberg reported that the removed stocks were linked to Indonesia’s richest people. Barito Renewables, Chandra Asri and Petrindo are controlled by the billionaire Prajogo Pangestu; Dian Swastatika is ultimately controlled by Franky Widjaja of the Sinar Mas group, according to Malay Mail.

In late October, the financial regulator, the Financial Services Authority (OJK), said it had penalised more than a dozen parties for manipulating trading, with record fines of 2.1 trillion rupiah, about $117 million. It said the fines had been paid but did not say who paid them. <mark>Being removed from an index, or named in a report, is not proof that a company or its owners did anything wrong;</mark> the regulator has not said who was sanctioned, and the companies’ shares remain listed and tradable.

## Why did a market problem become an economic crisis?

MSCI’s complaint is about how the market works. But the market’s fall, from a record in January to a six-year low in October, also reflects a year in which almost everything else went wrong for Indonesian assets at once.

### The oil shock and the rupiah

<Sidenote term="Rupiah">Indonesia’s currency. A weaker rupiah means more rupiah are needed to buy one US dollar, so imports, foreign debt payments and dividends paid to foreigners become dearer.</Sidenote>

Indonesia imports much of its fuel. When the Iran war began on 28 February and oil prices jumped (see issue 009), Indonesia needed far more dollars to pay for energy. Its trade surplus, which normally supplies those dollars, collapsed to just $89 million in April from $3.3 billion the month before. “Dollar supply from goods trade is dwindling, while dollar needs for energy imports, raw materials, dividends, foreign debt payments and seasonality needs remain significant,” Josua Pardede of Bank Permata told AFP in June.

At the same time, foreign investors were selling Indonesian shares and converting the proceeds into dollars. On 4 June the rupiah fell through 18,000 to the dollar for the first time, and on 8 June it hit a record low of about 18,190. It has been close to those lows ever since.

### A central bank under pressure

<Sidenote term="Central bank independence">The principle that a central bank should set interest rates to keep inflation and the currency stable, free from pressure by politicians who may want cheaper money before an election. Investors watch it closely, because a central bank seen as taking orders tends to mean a weaker currency.</Sidenote>

Bank Indonesia responded as central banks under currency pressure usually do. It raised interest rates by half a percentage point in May, then in a surprise move on 9 June, then again on 18 June, to 5.75%. It said the aim included raising yields “to attract foreign portfolio investment inflows to Indonesia.” It also spent reserves and, from May, made anyone buying more than $25,000 a month provide supporting documents.

But in June, parliament passed a law that gave the central bank responsibility for economic growth and job creation, and gave parliament a role in judging its performance. On 27 July its respected governor, Perry Warjiyo, resigned about two years before his term ended, citing personal reasons. Capital Economics and others speculated that he had been pressed to support the government’s push for growth. His deputy, Destry Damayanti, was confirmed as governor on 1 September as the only candidate; Fitch Ratings said her nomination reinforced “expectations of policy continuity”. A nephew of President Prabowo Subianto, Thomas Djiwandono, is a deputy governor.

On 21 October, after the MSCI shock, economists were split almost evenly on whether the bank would raise rates again. It held its main rate at 5.75%, raised the rate it pays banks on deposits to 5%, and cut the share of deposits that banks must hold at the central bank, releasing money into the economy. Analysts at Capital Economics warned that such mixed messages risk undermining the bank’s credibility.

### Spending, and who is in charge of the money

President Prabowo, who took office in October 2024, has big spending plans. The flagship is a free nutritious meals programme for schoolchildren and pregnant women, budgeted at 335 trillion rupiah (about $21 billion) for 2026, aimed eventually at 82.9 million people. The 2026 budget planned a deficit of 2.48% of GDP, close to the legal limit of 3%. In March, after the oil shock pushed up the cost of fuel subsidies, the government said it would scale the meals programme down for “fiscal efficiency”.

<Photo file="Wisma Danantara Indonesia (2025) 01.jpg" alt="An office building in Jakarta with the name Danantara Indonesia on its facade" caption="The headquarters of Danantara Indonesia, the sovereign wealth fund created in February 2025, photographed in July 2025." />

In February 2025 Prabowo also created Danantara, a sovereign wealth fund that is taking control of the government’s stakes in state companies such as the biggest banks, the oil company Pertamina and the power company PLN. Critics worry about how independent it is from the president, and Transparency International Indonesia has reported deputy ministers sitting on state companies’ boards.

None of these is the reason MSCI gave. But together they explain why investors were already nervous, and why MSCI’s verdict landed on a market with no cushion. In June, students marched in Jakarta and other cities under the slogan “Indonesia going bankrupt”, angry about the rupiah, fuel prices and corruption allegations in the meals programme.

## Who loses if Indonesia is demoted?

## Why did it come to this?

The obvious answer is a few tycoons gaming a market. That is part of it, but it does not explain why it was possible for so long, why it was tolerated, or why it came to a head in 2026.

<WhyList>
<Why title="Ownership in Indonesia is concentrated by history">

Indonesia’s big business groups grew up under President Suharto, whose New Order government ruled from 1966 to 1998, when access to licences, credit and state contracts depended on connections. Families built sprawling conglomerates that span mining, plantations, property, banks and energy. When they list companies, they usually list a slice and keep control. Concentrated ownership is not unusual in Asia, and it is not illegal. But it means many of the largest stocks on the exchange have only a small public slice, which is the raw material for both a thin float and an easily moved price.

</Why>
<Why title="The rules made it cheap to look public">

A 7.5% minimum float and a 5% disclosure threshold set a low bar. A company could meet the free float rule on paper while friendly holders, each under 5%, held much of the “public” float out of sight. The rules were not secret, but it took MSCI’s January freeze for regulators to double the float requirement within days.

</Why>
<Why title="Index rules rewarded size, and size could be manufactured">

Index providers mostly include the biggest companies. In a thin market, price is the easiest way to become big. Once a stock is in the index, buyers arrive automatically. That created an incentive, which the “MSCI trade” describes, to push valuations up to win a place in the index. MSCI’s own rules were part of the machine it is now complaining about, and it has spent this year tightening how it measures Indonesian free float.

</Why>
<Why title="Enforcement was weak, and the regulators were close to the regulated">

Manipulation rules exist in Indonesian law. The question is whether anyone enforces them against powerful people. Until October, Indonesia had never fined anyone as heavily as it now has, and even now it has not named those it fined. Bloomberg listed weak enforcement alongside opacity among MSCI’s concerns. In a political system where the biggest business families are close to the people in power, including the president’s own family, the incentive to pursue them hard was limited.

</Why>
<Why title="A confidence shock arrived at the same time">

The oil shock, falling reserves, a central bank whose governor quit early and a big spending programme gave foreign investors other reasons to leave. Each fall in the market made the governance problem more expensive, and each governance headline made the economic worries worse.

</Why>
</WhyList>

<Answer title="So is it corruption, incompetence or economics?">

Mostly institutional design, with a dose of politics. Indonesia built a stock market that looked open and modern, with millions of investors and a place in the world’s most important index, on top of an ownership structure that left the real power, and most of the shares, with a few families. The rules for free float and disclosure were weak enough that a company could look public without being so, and enforcement against powerful owners was rare.

That is not mainly a story of secret criminality; much of it was legal and visible. It is a story of rules written for a smaller, sleepier market that were never updated as global index money arrived, and of authorities who moved only when an outsider forced them to. The economic and political shocks of 2026 did not cause the problem, but they removed the patience that had let it continue.

</Answer>

## What can Indonesia’s government do?

The government’s problem is unusual: the judge is a private company in New York, the standard is investor trust, and the deadline is June 2027. MSCI has said what it wants to see, “clear evidence of material improvements in market governance and functioning”, but not exactly what would count. Indonesia has to convince sceptical investors, not pass a checklist.

### What has already been done

<Rows>
  <Row label="More shares in public hands">The minimum free float has been doubled, from 7.5% to 15%. Companies that fall short must sell shares or face consequences, which means owners selling into a falling market.</Row>
  <Row label="Seeing who owns what">The threshold for disclosing a shareholding has been cut from 5% to 1%, and the exchange has released more ownership data.</Row>
  <Row label="Cleaning up the benchmark">The exchange announced on 19 October that it will drop all stocks with high shareholding concentration from the Jakarta Composite, starting with 80 stocks worth about 18% of the index. They stay tradable, but their prices will no longer move the benchmark. The exchange is also adding outside experts to the committee that decides which stocks are labelled.</Row>
  <Row label="Punishing manipulation">Record fines of 2.1 trillion rupiah, and warnings from the president’s brother that the government “intends to take firm action”.</Row>
  <Row label="Supporting prices">Danantara has said it will buy more shares in listed state companies. “If the stocks are good, we will buy them,” said its chief operating officer, Dony Oskaria.</Row>
</Rows>

### What is hard

**Credibility is the real test.** MSCI said in October that reforms had been announced but sufficient progress had not been made. Announcing a rule is easy; investors want to see it enforced against a powerful family. That is why naming those fined, pursuing criminal cases where the evidence supports them, and giving the regulator visible independence matter more than another round of rules. Felix Darmawan of BCA Sekuritas said the June 2027 deadline “gives the securities regulator some time to clean house”.

**Every fix has a cost.** Forcing owners to sell shares to meet the float rule adds supply to a market that is already falling. Dropping tightly held stocks from the index makes the benchmark more honest but hurts the investors who own them. Raising interest rates to protect the rupiah slows growth just as the government is trying to speed it up.

**State support can make the problem worse.** If Danantara buys shares to prop up prices, it adds a large, politically controlled buyer to a market whose problem is that prices do not reflect real trading. Investors may welcome it in a panic and then ask whether the state is now doing what the tycoons were accused of.

**Some of it is outside the market regulator’s reach.** The oil shock, the dollar, the budget and the central bank’s credibility all feed into how foreign investors see Indonesia, and none of them is controlled by the exchange or the OJK. A clean market in a country whose currency is falling and whose central bank is seen as taking orders will still struggle to attract money.

**And MSCI is not the only judge.** FTSE Russell, the other big index provider, has not acted yet. Goldman estimates a further $2.7 billion of selling if it does. Reforms aimed only at MSCI’s wording will not be enough if other investors still doubt the market.

<Answer title="So what can the government actually do?">

It can make the market harder to game: more public shares, full disclosure, real penalties for powerful people, and a benchmark that excludes stocks whose prices cannot be trusted. Most of that is already announced. What it cannot do quickly is make investors believe it, and that depends on enforcement it has rarely shown, on a central bank seen as independent, and on a budget that does not keep testing the 3% limit. The next eight months are less about new rules than about proof.

</Answer>

## Where do reasonable people disagree?

<Debate topic="On whether MSCI is right to punish Indonesia this hard">
  <Side title="Yes, the market had to be told the truth">Index providers exist to give investors a fair picture. If investors cannot trust a market’s prices or know who owns its companies, calling it an emerging market misleads the millions of people whose pensions track the index. Indonesia had nine months of warnings and moved only when forced. MSCI says investors told it a frontier label would understate the problem. A harsh signal is what finally produced record fines and real rule changes; a softer one would have let the reforms stall again.</Side>
  <Side title="No, it is too blunt and too powerful">A private company is putting a $1 trillion market, with millions of ordinary investors, in the same category as Lebanon, punishing well-run banks and consumer firms for the conduct of a few tightly held stocks. The reforms are real and recent and need time to work. Mohit Mirpuri of SGMC Capital said in June that MSCI’s concerns highlighted “areas to fix” but did not “build a credible case” even for frontier status. And MSCI’s own index rules helped create the incentive to inflate tightly held stocks in the first place.</Side>
</Debate>

<Debate topic="On whether the state should step in to support the market">
  <Side title="Step in to stop a panic">Markets can overshoot. When forced index selling and fear drive good companies far below their value, a large patient buyer such as Danantara can steady prices, protect ordinary investors and buy assets cheaply for the nation. Governments from Hong Kong in 1998 to China more recently have done it. A calmer market also gives reforms time to work.</Side>
  <Side title="Stay out and fix the rules">The problem MSCI identified is that Indonesian prices do not reflect genuine trading. A politically controlled fund buying shares to hold up prices adds another reason to doubt them, and turns state money into a safety net for the owners who benefited from the boom. Better to let prices find their level, enforce the rules and let investors return when they trust what they see.</Side>

## Has this happened to other markets before?

Index downgrades are rare, and each one has followed a loss of trust in how money moves or how markets work. MSCI cut Greece from developed to emerging status in 2013, after its debt crisis. It moved Pakistan from emerging to frontier status in 2021. It demoted Argentina to standalone status in 2021 because of capital controls that made it hard for investors to get money out, and Nigeria and Lebanon sit in the standalone group for similar reasons today.

Indonesia’s case is different in one important way: its problem is not that foreigners cannot get their money out, but that they cannot trust what they are buying. That makes it closer to the reasons MSCI also downgraded Turkey’s information flow score this year. The closest Indonesian precedent is the Asian financial crisis of 1997–98, when the rupiah collapsed, the stock market crashed and Suharto fell. The fall on 16 October was the biggest since January 1998. This time the banks are not failing and the economy is still growing, but the lesson investors drew in 1998, that connections mattered more than rules, is the one Indonesia now has to prove wrong.
