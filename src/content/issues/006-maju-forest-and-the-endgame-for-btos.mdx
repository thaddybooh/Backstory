<Overview>

On 10 July 2026, Singapore’s Housing and Development Board (HDB), which builds the flats that about three in four households live in, announced plans for two new housing estates on forested land. One is at Sunset Way in Clementi, in the west of the island, where about 15 of the 23 hectares of a young forest known as Maju Forest would be cleared, leaving about 8 hectares as a wildlife refuge. The other is at Gillman Barracks, a former British army camp turned art district in the south, where at least 10 hectares of forest could go. Both sites have been zoned for housing in government plans since 1980.

The reaction was unusually strong for Singapore. More than 49,000 people signed two online petitions asking the government to build on already-developed land, such as golf courses and old industrial sites, instead. Around 18 questions were filed in Parliament. On 16 August about 2,000 people, by the organisers’ count, gathered at Hong Lim Park, the only place in Singapore where citizens can hold a demonstration without a police permit. Two days before the rally the government said it would keep more of both forests than planned, and said plainly that this means fewer homes. As of late October, the final plans had not been published.

The fight is about more than one forest. Singapore is an island of about 740 square kilometres with no countryside to expand into, a growing number of households and a promise that most citizens can buy a new, subsidised flat. Those flats are sold mainly through the Build-To-Order (BTO) system. This issue explains what is being cleared and why, who the new flats are for, how the BTO model works, and the question underneath it all: on a fixed amount of land, how long can new homes stay both plentiful and affordable, and what has to give?

</Overview>

## What is actually being cleared

### Maju Forest

<Sidenote term="Secondary forest">Forest that has grown back on land that was once cleared, for farms, plantations or railways. Almost all of Singapore’s forest outside its nature reserves is secondary.</Sidenote>

Maju Forest is not ancient rainforest. It is a **secondary forest** about 50 years old, which grew on abandoned plantation land and the old railway corridor. That is typical: Singapore lost almost all of its original forest under British rule, and the green patches outside its protected reserves are mostly land that was cleared and then left alone. Secondary forest covers about 16% of the island, according to figures reported by the environmental news site Mongabay.

Young does not mean empty. The environmental impact assessment for Sunset Way, carried out by the consultancy Aurecon for the National Parks Board (NParks) on behalf of HDB, recorded 286 plant species, 49 of them of conservation significance, including five critically endangered wild tree species. It counted 1,661 trees and 113 animal species, six of them of conservation significance: the buffy fish-owl, the straw-headed bulbul (a songbird that is critically endangered worldwide because it is trapped for the caged-bird trade), the changeable hawk-eagle, the oriental magpie-robin, the bamboo bat and the long-tailed macaque. Some news reports say government studies also recorded the critically endangered Sunda pangolin; the Sunset Way summary does not list it.

<Sidenote term="Environmental impact assessment (EIA)">A study of what a project will do to plants, animals, water and soil, and how the harm could be reduced. In Singapore, EIAs for these sites are commissioned by government agencies and published for comment.</Sidenote>

The study’s most important finding is about connections. The site links to the much larger Clementi Forest next door through the old railway line, now a walking trail called the Rail Corridor, which lets animals move between forest patches. The July plan kept about 8 hectares, mostly along the forest edges: the southern strip along the old railway, a freshwater stream with a buffer around it, and part of the most valuable native forest. The rest, largely abandoned-land forest and forest dominated by a fast-growing introduced tree called *Albizia*, would become housing. A new MRT (metro) station on the Cross Island Line, Maju station, is already being built at the site’s north-east corner.

### Gillman Barracks

Gillman Barracks is a 40-hectare site in the south of the island, built as a British army camp in the 1930s and used in recent years for art galleries. HDB plans a mix of public and private housing there. A heritage study assessed 86 buildings: all four rated “exceptional” would be kept, along with 21 of the 27 rated “high”. Its environmental study found 293 plant and 178 animal species. HDB proposed keeping most of the native forest and a stream, with green corridors at least 30 metres wide linking nearby parks, for about 8 hectares of continuous green space. In the original plan, green space equal to about 40% of the site’s current area would remain.

<Photo file="Gillman Barracks Block 9.jpg" alt="A white colonial-era barrack building used as art galleries at Gillman Barracks" caption="Art galleries in a former barrack building at Gillman Barracks, photographed in 2017. All four buildings rated of “exceptional” heritage value would be kept." />

### What changed after the backlash

Two things stand out about the government’s response. First, it moved: within five weeks it promised to keep more greenery at both sites, working with nature groups that had suggested wider green corridors. Second, it did not move on the basic question. Asked in Parliament by Workers’ Party Non-Constituency MP Andre Low whether not building was an option, Tan said both sites are zoned for housing, so “we will proceed, but how we build is important.” He also set out the trade-off that frames the whole debate:

<Quote cite="Alvin Tan, Minister of State for National Development, Parliament, 4 August 2026">We’ve already reduced the number of homes to retain important green and heritage areas. If we conserve even more, we will build even fewer homes.</Quote>

Critics say this shows the consultation was about how much to clear, not whether. Mongabay reported that Singapore has never rejected a forest development because of an EIA, though public feedback has led to parts of sites being kept. The government says the studies are “not meant to justify a plan that is already fixed” but are part of shaping it, in line with a principle Tan summarised as: avoid harm where possible, reduce it where not, and mitigate what remains.

## Why build on forest at all?

Singapore’s government frames the issue as a matter of arithmetic. The island is about 740 square kilometres, roughly the size of New York City’s five boroughs together, and already about a quarter larger than at independence in 1965 because of land reclamation. On that land it has to fit housing for more than six million people, the ports, airports and industry that the economy depends on, water catchments, military training areas and nature. In 2020, according to Urban Redevelopment Authority (URA) data reported by Reuters, about 14% of the land was used for housing and 9.3% for parks and nature reserves.

<Sidenote term="Greenfield and brownfield">A greenfield site has never been built on, or has gone back to nature. A brownfield site has been built on before, such as an old factory, school or golf course, and can be redeveloped.</Sidenote>

The petitioners’ main argument is that Singapore should build on **brownfield** land first: land already built on or heavily managed. The government says it does. Tan pointed to large brownfield projects: moving Paya Lebar Air Base, which will free a large area in the east; consolidating the port at Tuas, which frees the old city ports for a new waterfront; the redevelopment of the old Keppel Club golf course into housing at Berlayar; and the former Bukit Timah racecourse at Turf City, where the URA plans 15,000 to 20,000 homes over the next 20 to 30 years. Older HDB estates in Clementi and Bukit Merah have already been rebuilt at higher density. Other spare sites near Maju Forest, such as a former junior college at Mount Sinai and an old bus depot at Ghim Moh, are already planned for housing.

The problem, HDB says, is timing and location. Many brownfield sites are not free yet: the air base will not move until the 2030s, and large sites take years to clear and plan. HDB’s chief town planner said suitable land within Clementi and neighbouring areas “had become increasingly limited”. And HDB wants new flats in established areas, not only on the edges: Sunset Way is meant particularly for young families who want to live near their parents in the west.

### What about the golf courses?

Golf courses are the petitioners’ favourite example, because they are large, green but heavily managed and used by relatively few, often wealthy, people. Singapore had 17 golf courses covering about 1,500 hectares in 2014, according to figures reported at the time, more than 60 times the size of Maju Forest.

The government has been taking some of that land back. In July 2025 the Ministry of Law said that of the 16 courses then operating, the leases of Warren Golf & Country Club and Orchid Country Club will end in 2030 and both sites are marked for housing. Two more courses whose leases end in 2030, the Sime course used by Keppel Club and the Bukit course of the Singapore Island Country Club, will become public golf courses instead. Tanah Merah’s Garden course, near Changi Airport, is being studied for airport-related uses after 2035. Tan told Parliament that more than 400 hectares of golf course land will be returned by 2030.

<Photo file="View of the Sime Course, one of the Singapore Island Country Club's golf courses, from Sime Road, Singapore - 20130728-01.JPG" alt="A wide green fairway of the Sime golf course, surrounded by trees" caption="The Sime golf course in central Singapore, photographed in 2013. Its lease ends in 2030; the government plans to keep it as a public course rather than build on it." />

So why not build on all of them, and leave the forests? There are real reasons and contested ones. Some courses sit in water catchments next to reservoirs, where building is restricted to protect the water supply; the Sime and Bukit courses are beside the Central Catchment Nature Reserve. Some, like Tanah Merah, are under flight paths with height limits. And the government has a policy of keeping some golf for residents and for business visitors. Critics answer that this is a choice about who gets to use land, not a law of nature, and that a few hundred hectares of fairways are worth more as homes than a few dozen hectares of young forest are as flats. That debate came to a head when a film director called the rally organisers’ focus on golf courses “politics of envy”, and drew a backlash.

### Not all green is equal

The government points out that Singapore is very green: Prime Minister Wong said in August that nearly half the island is covered in greenery, and a national movement aims to plant a million extra trees by 2030 (NParks says more than 887,000 have been planted since 2020). It is also planning about 1,000 hectares of new parks and green spaces over the next 10 to 15 years.

Ecologists say this mixes up different kinds of green. A headline figure that includes roadside trees, rooftop gardens and lawns is not the same as forest. “Not all green spaces are equal,” Rachel Oh, a conservation researcher at the National University of Singapore, told Reuters: mature forests cool the area around them and soak up rain in ways that new parks take decades to match. Winston Chow, an urban climate scientist at Singapore Management University, told Mongabay that clearing forests like Maju would raise local temperatures and flood risk. A 2023 study by Yitong Wu, which laid the URA’s 2019 master plan over maps of unprotected forest, estimated that up to 7,331 hectares of secondary forest, more than half of what remains, could be lost to planned development: 1.2 times the area of all of Singapore’s parks and nature reserves. The government says not every forested site marked for development will be cleared.

## Who wants the new flats?

If Singapore’s birth rate is among the lowest in the world, why does it need so many new homes? Tan answered this in Parliament. Today’s and tomorrow’s flat buyers were born 25 to 35 years ago, when birth rates were higher, so a falling birth rate now does not cut demand for decades. More importantly, **households** are multiplying faster than people.

The Department of Statistics counted about 1.49 million resident households in 2025, up 1.6% in a year. The average household has shrunk from 3.39 people in 2015 to 3.06. One-person households rose from 11.9% to 16.7% of the total in the same decade, to about 248,000. More young couples want to live apart from their parents, more single people want their own flats, older people are living longer in their own homes, and the population, including permanent residents, has grown. Fewer people per home means more homes for the same population.

<Sidenote term="Build-To-Order (BTO)">HDB’s main way of selling new flats. It announces projects, takes applications, holds a ballot, and starts building once enough flats are booked. Buyers usually wait three to five years for the keys.</Sidenote>

### Demand is high, but in particular places

The picture from recent BTO sales is more mixed than “everyone is queuing”. HDB has been building fast after a backlog caused by the Covid-19 pandemic: about 55,000 BTO flats from 2025 to 2027, with 19,600 in 2026 alone across sales in February, June and October. Waiting times are coming down; more than 4,000 flats this year have waits of three years or less.

That extra supply has cooled overall demand. In February 2026, first-time family applicants for three-room and larger flats had a median of 0.8 applicants per flat, meaning many projects had more flats than first-time families applying, down from 1.9 in October 2025. In June 2026 the figure was 1.3, which National Development Minister Chee Hong Tat called stable. In February, some four- and five-room flats in Sembawang, in the far north, drew fewer than one first-time family applicant for every two flats.

But demand is extremely uneven. In June’s exercise, first-time families applied 3.7 times over for four-room flats at Lakeview Cascadia in Bishan, a central, established town; second-timer families, who already had one subsidised flat and get a smaller quota, applied 17.2 times over. Single people, who can buy only small two-room flats, faced a median of 7.3 applicants per flat and 18.1 at one Woodlands project. Tan noted that the recent projects in Clementi and Bukit Merah, Clementi Emerald and Berlayar Rise, were each oversubscribed about four times.

<mark>The shortage is not of flats in general but of flats in the places people most want to live: near the city, near their parents, near good schools and transport.</mark> That is exactly where the remaining forests at Sunset Way and Gillman are. It also explains why the government is reluctant to simply build more in far-off new towns: it has done that, and the flats there are getting fewer applicants.

## How the BTO model works, and what it promises

To understand what is at stake, it helps to know how a BTO flat is priced, because it is unlike almost any other housing system.

<Chain>

1. HDB starts with the **market value**: what a similar resale flat nearby sells for, adjusted for being new.
2. It then takes off a large **subsidy**, which is bigger in popular, central areas where market values are higher. The price is not based on what the flat cost to build.
3. First-time buyers can get further **grants** of up to S$80,000, depending on income, through the Enhanced CPF Housing Grant.
4. Most buyers pay with money from their **CPF accounts**, the compulsory savings scheme, so many need little or no cash.
5. HDB pays the government the **full market value of the land**. That money goes into Singapore’s protected past reserves, not into the annual budget.
6. Because flats sell below cost, HDB runs a **deficit** on each project, which the government covers from the budget.

</Chain>

<Sidenote term="Past reserves">Savings built up by previous governments, protected by the constitution. The current government cannot spend them without the elected President’s approval. State land counts as part of these reserves.</Sidenote>

The deficit is large. In the year to March 2025, HDB lost about S$6.3 billion before government grants, and the government gave it S$6.35 billion to cover the gap. A single large project, Central Weave in Ang Mo Kio, was estimated to lose about S$250 million.

The point about land is central and contested. The government argues that state land belongs to the reserves, built up by past generations; selling it cheaply to today’s buyers would quietly spend those reserves. Critics, including the Progress Singapore Party, have argued that BTO flats could be priced much lower if land were counted at a lower value, since the money only moves from one government pocket to another. The government’s answer is that land sold below value cannot be sold again, and that pricing flats on cost would make them cheapest in the best locations, handing the luckiest buyers the biggest windfall.

### What the model promises

The promise of the BTO model is that ordinary working families can buy a new home, and that it will grow in value. The Ministry of National Development (MND) says first-time buyers’ BTO flats typically cost four to five times their annual household income, and that most can pay their monthly loans entirely from CPF savings. Between 2012 and 2021, its figures show, the median price of four-room BTO flats in non-mature estates bought by first-time families rose 10% before grants while those buyers’ incomes rose 27%.

<Sidenote term="Minimum occupation period (MOP)">How long owners must live in their flat before they can sell it. Five years for standard flats, ten for Plus and Prime flats.</Sidenote>

The promise has a catch. A flat bought with a large subsidy can, after the **minimum occupation period** (MOP), be sold at the resale market price. In central areas that has produced well-publicised windfalls, including resale flats sold for more than a million Singapore dollars. Since October 2024 HDB has sorted new flats into **Standard**, **Plus** and **Prime**. Plus and Prime flats, in more desirable locations, get bigger subsidies but must be lived in for ten years instead of five, cannot be rented out whole, and when sold the owner must pay back part of the price (6% to 9% of the resale price, as of late 2024). The idea is that if central land is going to be used for subsidised flats, buyers should not be able to cash in on it.

That classification matters for Maju Forest and Gillman. Both are in or near established, popular areas, so flats there would probably be Plus or Prime: heavily subsidised, tightly restricted and aimed at people who want to live there, not at investors.

### The resale market and the 99-year lease

The other half of the system is the resale market, where owners sell flats to other eligible buyers at market prices. Resale prices rose very fast after the pandemic, by 9.6% in 2024 alone, as construction delays held back new supply. They have since levelled off: HDB’s flash estimate showed a 0.2% fall in the third quarter of 2026, the third quarterly fall in a row, as tens of thousands of new flats were completed.

<Sidenote term="Lease decay">The fall in a flat’s value as its 99-year lease runs down, because at the end of the lease the flat returns to the state with nothing paid to the owner.</Sidenote>

Every HDB flat comes with a **99-year lease**. When it ends, the flat and land go back to the state, and the owner receives nothing. For decades this hardly mattered, because the leases were young. It matters more now. The oldest HDB blocks still standing are reaching 60, and their prices are beginning to reflect **lease decay**. The government’s main answer is the Voluntary Early Redevelopment Scheme (VERS), announced in 2018: when blocks are about 70 years old, residents may vote on whether the government should buy them back early and redevelop the land. The terms will be less generous than older schemes, and details have still not been published; in November 2025 the government said it would consult the public first.

## The endgame: what happens when the land runs out?

Put the pieces together and the long-term problem becomes clear. The BTO model depends on three things: a steady supply of land the state can build on, buyers who can afford what is built, and a government willing to pay the gap. Each is under pressure.

**Land.** Singapore has been finding new land in four ways: clearing secondary forest, reclaiming land from the sea, recycling brownfield sites, and building higher. Forest clearance is becoming politically costly, as Maju Forest shows. Reclamation is expensive, slow and harms coral reefs and coastlines; in August the prime minister announced plans to merge several southern islands into one large Western Island over “the coming decades”, and a 800-hectare “Long Island” off the east coast is under study. Brownfield land arrives in big batches, but only when leases expire and facilities move.

**Density.** Building higher and denser on old land is the main remaining lever. It is how the old Keppel Club and Turf City sites will be used, and how older estates were rebuilt in the past. VERS is meant, in part, to recycle the land under the oldest estates in the 2030s and beyond. But there are limits: height limits near airports and air bases, the cost of rebuilding, and residents’ dislike of crowding.

**Price.** As land gets scarcer and costlier, the gap between market value and the subsidised BTO price grows, and so do HDB’s deficits. The government can keep covering them, but every dollar comes from the same budget that must pay for an ageing population (see issue 004). Or it can let BTO prices rise faster, which tests the promise that new flats stay within reach.

**Fairness between generations.** Today’s owners benefit when resale prices rise. Tomorrow’s buyers benefit when they don’t. A system that treats the flat both as a home and as most families’ main retirement asset is caught between those two goals. The Plus and Prime rules, the income ceiling rise from S$14,000 to S$16,000 a month announced in August, and the new extra ballot chances for families with children from February 2027 are all attempts to steer scarce flats to the people the government thinks need them most.

## Why the government keeps choosing forests

Why does Singapore, a rich country that brands itself a “City in Nature”, keep planning homes on its forests? It is tempting to call it a lack of care for nature, or a government that doesn’t listen. The fuller answer is a set of structural pressures, some of them choices.

<WhyList>
<Why title="The land supply is fixed and already crowded">

Almost everything on the island is already assigned to a use, and many uses (airports, ports, water catchments, military land) cannot be squeezed. Secondary forest on land zoned for housing decades ago is, in planners’ terms, land waiting to be used. From that point of view, keeping it is the exception that needs justifying, not clearing it.

</Why>
<Why title="Demand is concentrated where the land is gone">

The places with the longest queues for flats are the older, central and western towns, where almost no empty land is left. Sunset Way and Gillman are among the last large open sites in those areas. Building more in distant new towns does not satisfy people who want to live near their parents or work.

</Why>
<Why title="Brownfield land comes too slowly">

Golf course leases, air bases and ports free up land in large blocks, but on fixed timetables years away. The government has promised shorter waits for flats now, after the post-pandemic backlog angered voters. Forests already zoned for housing are ready sooner.

</Why>
<Why title="The planning system is built to decide from the top">

Land use is decided by government agencies through a Master Plan reviewed every five years. Environmental studies are commissioned by the same agencies that want to build, and are not legally binding. The public is consulted on how to build, rarely on whether. That makes it efficient at delivering homes, and also makes forest loss the default when interests clash.

</Why>
<Why title="Forests’ value is hard to put a price on">

Homes have a clear value: buyers, prices and political credit. A forest’s cooling, flood control, wildlife and enjoyment are real but rarely counted in the decision. Campaigners want these “ecosystem services” added to the cost-benefit sums; the government says it weighs them but has not published how.

</Why>
<Why title="Some land is protected by policy choices, not physics">

Golf courses, large landed-housing estates and other low-density uses take up far more land than Maju Forest. Some is constrained for real reasons, such as water catchments and flight paths. But how much land is kept for golf or for bungalows is a political decision, and critics argue it shows whose interests the system protects.

</Why>
</WhyList>

<Answer title="So is it greed, bad planning or simple arithmetic?">

Mostly arithmetic, shaped by choices. Singapore really is short of land, and its forests sit where demand for housing is strongest. Nobody is profiting privately from clearing Maju Forest; the flats at Sunset Way will be sold below cost. But the arithmetic is not neutral. It treats forests zoned decades ago as spare land, counts homes more easily than cooling or wildlife, and leaves some golf courses and low-density estates largely untouched.

**What has to give?** On a fixed island, something always does: forest, sea, existing low-density uses, density in older estates, or the price and speed of new flats. The Maju Forest fight is the first time large numbers of Singaporeans have argued publicly about which of those it should be.

</Answer>

## What this means for Singapore’s government

The policy problem is that the government has promised three things that pull against each other on a small island: plenty of new, affordable homes in the places people want to live; a green “City in Nature”; and a growing economy with the land it needs. A few hectares of forest at a time, it has been trading the second for the first. Maju Forest suggests the political cost of that trade is rising. The main options, and why each is hard:

<Rows>
  <Row label="Build on golf and low-density land first">Faster recovery of golf course land and more housing on large landed estates would ease pressure on forests. But leases are contracts with fixed end dates, some courses sit in water catchments or under flight paths, and taking land from wealthy residents and clubs early would be costly and politically fraught.</Row>
  <Row label="Build higher and denser">More homes per hectare on existing housing land, through redevelopment and taller blocks. This is the main long-term lever, but VERS will only start in the 2030s, rebuilding is expensive, and residents often object to crowding and construction.</Row>
  <Row label="Make more land">Reclamation and island merging add land but take decades, cost a great deal, need sand from abroad and destroy coastal and reef habitats. They do not help the queue for flats in Clementi this decade.</Row>
  <Row label="Protect more forest by law">Campaigners want the most valuable forests made permanent nature parks and EIAs made legally required before plans are drawn up. That would give certainty but reduce the government’s flexibility, and every protected hectare is land unavailable for other needs.</Row>
  <Row label="Steer demand">Income ceilings, priority schemes, Plus and Prime rules and singles’ eligibility all decide who gets scarce flats in popular areas. Tightening them eases queues but leaves some people waiting longer; loosening them, as in August, increases demand.</Row>
  <Row label="Accept higher costs">Paying more to build on brownfield or reclaimed land, or to build more densely, means bigger HDB deficits or higher prices. Either competes with spending on an ageing population.</Row>
</Rows>

There are also problems of trust and process. Residents and nature groups have complained that they could not see estimates of future housing need or the number of homes planned at each site; HDB said it could not yet give a housing yield for Gillman. The organisers of the rally asked the government to publish its housing need projections and explain why each alternative site was rejected. Publishing more would make the trade-offs easier to judge, but also easier to contest.

What is actually on the table now is incremental: revised plans for both sites that keep more forest and build fewer homes, a report summarising how agencies responded to public feedback, continued high BTO supply, golf course land returning from 2030, and very long-term reclamation. The government has not said it will change the rules for how forested land zoned for housing is assessed.

## Where reasonable people disagree

<Debate topic="On whether to clear Maju Forest">
  <Side title="Build the homes">Singapore has very little land and many young families waiting for flats in established towns. Maju Forest is young secondary forest on land zoned for housing since 1980, and the plan keeps the most valuable parts and the wildlife corridor. Every hectare not built on here must be found somewhere else, often further from where people want to live, and brownfield land will not be ready in time. Housing is a basic need; a forest that grew on abandoned land 50 years ago should not outrank it.</Side>
  <Side title="Keep the forest">Forests are not spare land. Once cleared they take generations to recover, and they cool, drain and connect a city in ways parks cannot. Singapore still has golf courses, low-density estates and redevelopable sites that could supply the same homes with less loss. Using up the last forests in older towns is a choice to take the cheapest path today, and future residents will pay through hotter streets and fewer wild places.</Side>
</Debate>

<Debate topic="On the long-term future of the BTO model">
  <Side title="The model can adapt">The BTO system has kept new homes within reach of most families for six decades, with prices tied to incomes and protected by subsidies. Plus and Prime rules, VERS, redevelopment and brownfield land give it the tools to keep going. Land is scarce, but Singapore has always found more of it through planning and reclamation.</Side>
  <Side title="The model needs rethinking">The system relies on ever-rising resale values to serve as people’s retirement savings, on cheap new land and on growing subsidies. As land runs short and leases decay, those cannot all continue. Singapore will eventually need to choose between flats as affordable homes and flats as appreciating assets, and to plan for higher density and more renting, rather than clearing its last forests to postpone the choice.</Side>
</Debate>

## Further context: from garden city to “City in Nature”

In 1967, Singapore’s first prime minister, Lee Kuan Yew, launched the idea of a “Garden City”: planting trees along every road to make a crowded, industrialising island pleasant to live in and attractive to foreign investors. Greenery became part of the national brand. In 2020 the government upgraded the slogan to “City in Nature” and promised to restore forests and connect them with green corridors. At the same time it kept building: towns such as Punggol and Tengah went up on former farms and forests. The 2021 dispute over Dover Forest, about 2 kilometres from Maju, was the first sign that the public no longer saw secondary forests as empty land; HDB kept half of it and promised to revisit the rest in about ten years. Maju Forest is the same argument, louder.

## Key terms

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="HDB">Housing and Development Board, the government agency that plans, builds and sells public housing, where about three-quarters of households live.</Term>
  <Term name="BTO">Build-To-Order, HDB’s system of selling new flats before they are built, by ballot.</Term>
  <Term name="Secondary forest">Forest that has regrown on land previously cleared.</Term>
  <Term name="EIA">Environmental impact assessment: a study of a project’s effects on the environment and how to reduce them.</Term>
  <Term name="Brownfield site">Land that has been built on before and can be redeveloped.</Term>
  <Term name="Master Plan">Singapore’s statutory land-use plan, reviewed every five years, which sets what each piece of land can be used for.</Term>
  <Term name="Plus and Prime flats">BTO flats in more desirable locations, with bigger subsidies and tighter rules on resale and renting.</Term>
  <Term name="MOP">Minimum occupation period: how long owners must live in a flat before selling it.</Term>
  <Term name="Lease decay">The fall in a flat’s value as its 99-year lease runs down.</Term>
  <Term name="VERS">Voluntary Early Redevelopment Scheme, a planned scheme letting residents of old estates vote for early government buyback and redevelopment.</Term>
</KeyTerms>

### Concepts worth learning properly

<Concepts>
  <Concept title="Opportunity cost">Every use of a piece of land rules out the others. The Maju debate is about which forgone use matters most.</Concept>
  <Concept title="Ecosystem services">The economic value of what nature does for free, such as cooling, flood control and pollination, and why it is often left out of decisions.</Concept>
  <Concept title="Land value capture">How governments that own land recover the value public investment creates, and how Singapore uses land sales and lease rules to do it.</Concept>
