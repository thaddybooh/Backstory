<Overview>

Every phone, laptop, games console, car and server needs memory chips: DRAM, the fast working memory a device uses while it runs, and NAND flash, which stores photos, apps and files. For decades these were among the cheapest, most interchangeable parts in electronics. In the past year they have become some of the most fought-over. Contract prices for ordinary DRAM rose by more than half in each of the first two quarters of 2026, according to the research firm TrendForce, and by most estimates the price of a typical memory chip is now three to four times what it was a year ago. Smartphone makers have raised prices and cut their cheapest models; IDC, a market research firm, expects global phone shipments to fall 16.7% this year, the largest drop on record.

The cause is artificial intelligence. The data centres being built by Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta, OpenAI and others need enormous amounts of memory, above all a premium stacked kind called high-bandwidth memory (HBM) that sits next to AI chips such as Nvidia’s. Only three companies make most of the world’s DRAM: South Korea’s Samsung and SK hynix and America’s Micron. They have moved their factories towards AI memory because it pays far more, and making HBM uses about three times as much factory capacity as ordinary memory for the same amount of storage. What is left for phones and PCs has shrunk, and the AI buyers, with the deepest pockets, have locked up much of the future supply in long-term contracts.

The result is one of the biggest profit booms in corporate history. On 28 October SK hynix reported an operating profit of 83.3 trillion won (about $62.8 billion) for three months, with 80 cents of every dollar of sales left over as operating profit. Samsung expects about $80 billion for the same quarter, more than any company has earned in a quarter except Saudi Aramco. The makers say the shortage will last until 2028 or even 2030. Critics, from PC makers to a group of American consumers suing the three companies, say they are profiting from scarcity they have chosen not to fix. And investors are asking the oldest question in the memory business: when does the boom turn into a bust?

</Overview>

## What memory chips are, and why AI needs so many

<Sidenote term="DRAM">Dynamic random-access memory. The fast, temporary memory a computer uses while it works. It forgets everything when the power goes off, which is why devices also need storage.</Sidenote>

<Sidenote term="NAND flash">The type of memory used for long-term storage in phones, USB sticks and solid-state drives (SSDs). Slower than DRAM, but it keeps data without power.</Sidenote>

Computers use two main kinds of memory chip. **DRAM** is the working memory: the space where a processor keeps whatever it is busy with right now. **NAND flash** is storage: where apps, photos and files live. Both are made by etching billions of tiny cells onto thin discs of silicon called wafers, in factories (“fabs”) that cost tens of billions of dollars, and both are commodities. A gigabyte of DRAM from one maker does the same job as one from another, so buyers shop on price and the makers compete mainly on cost and scale.

For most of the last decade, the biggest buyers were phone and PC makers. A phone might hold 8 to 12 gigabytes of DRAM; a laptop 16. AI changed the scale of demand. A large AI model is, at bottom, billions of numbers that must sit in memory and be read again and again, very quickly, while the model is trained and every time someone uses it. The more powerful AI chips have become, the more the limit on their speed has become memory: how much they can hold, and how fast they can be fed. CLSA, an investment bank, cites estimates that about 60% of the energy in AI data centres goes on moving data rather than computing with it.

### High-bandwidth memory

<Sidenote term="High-bandwidth memory (HBM)">DRAM chips stacked on top of each other, wired together vertically and placed right next to a processor, so that data can flow between them much faster than through ordinary memory.</Sidenote>

The answer the industry found is **high-bandwidth memory**. Instead of placing memory chips on a separate board, an HBM “stack” piles 8, 12 or 16 DRAM chips on top of each other, links them with thousands of tiny vertical wires drilled through the silicon, and places the stack right next to the AI processor. The newest version, HBM4, can move up to about 2 terabytes of data a second per stack. Nvidia’s AI chips each carry several stacks, and a single rack of Nvidia’s latest servers holds 72 of those chips.

<Photo file="AMD Fiji GPU package with GPU, HBM memory and interposer.jpg" alt="A computer chip package with a large square processor in the middle flanked by four smaller rectangular memory stacks" caption="The first graphics chip to use HBM, made by AMD and photographed in 2015: the processor in the centre, with four memory stacks beside it. Today’s AI chips use the same basic layout with far more memory." />

HBM is hard to make. Each chip in the stack is larger than an ordinary DRAM chip, and more of them come out faulty. The industry’s rule of thumb is that making a given amount of HBM uses about three times as many wafers as the same amount of ordinary DRAM, and the ratio is higher for the newest generation. <mark>Every wafer a maker turns into HBM is roughly three wafers’ worth of ordinary memory that phones and PCs don’t get.</mark>

### How much AI is buying

AI is not only using HBM. The ordinary servers that support AI services also need large amounts of conventional DRAM, and AI companies are buying huge numbers of fast solid-state drives to store data. According to TrendForce, HBM and high-capacity server memory together account for about half of all DRAM bits supplied in 2026. Big cloud companies are expected to roughly double their capital spending this year, and TrendForce estimates that memory will make up 47% of that spending in 2026 and 68% in 2027, simply because memory has become so expensive.

The single most striking signal came in October 2025, when OpenAI signed letters of intent with Samsung and SK hynix for its $500 billion Stargate data centre project. The companies said Stargate could eventually need up to 900,000 DRAM wafers a month, which SK said was more than double the industry’s whole HBM capacity at the time; Tom’s Hardware calculated it at up to 40% of global DRAM output. The letters were not binding and had no timetable. But they told the industry that demand on a scale nobody had planned for was coming.

## How the shortage spread

The shortage started at the top of the market and worked its way down.

<Chain>

1. **Makers move capacity to AI.** Samsung, SK hynix and Micron move their newest production lines and most of their new investment towards HBM and high-capacity server memory, which earn far more per wafer.
2. **Ordinary memory gets scarce.** With fewer wafers left for conventional DRAM, makers prioritise cloud companies, which are “locking in capacity”, according to TrendForce. Suppliers’ own stocks fall to historic lows.
3. **Prices jump.** Conventional DRAM contract prices rise 55–60% in January–March 2026 and 58–63% in April–June. NAND flash follows, as makers shift it to data-centre drives. TrendForce expects server DRAM prices to rise about 270% over 2026 as a whole.
4. **Older chips get squeezed too.** As the big three exit older products such as DDR4, buyers who need them, from carmakers to makers of routers and TVs, bid up whatever is left. TrendForce reports shortages reaching even DDR2, a product first sold two decades ago.
5. **Device makers pay or cut back.** Phone and PC makers raise prices, put less memory in their products, drop their cheapest models, or run down their stocks and wait.
6. **Consumers buy less.** Higher prices mean fewer sales, especially in poorer countries and at the cheap end of the market.

</Chain>

### Who gets squeezed

**Phones.** Memory makes up 15–20% of the cost of the parts in a mid-range phone, according to IDC, and the cost of DRAM and NAND for phones is up more than 300% on a year earlier. IDC now expects smartphone shipments to fall 16.7% in 2026, to just over a billion, while the average price rises 27.6% to $581. The pain is concentrated at the bottom. Shipments of phones under $100 fell almost 60% in April–June compared with a year before. Android phone shipments are forecast to fall 24.3%; Apple’s iPhone, sold at higher prices with supply secured further ahead, only 1.3%. In emerging markets, IDC expects sales to fall by more than 20%. “The era of the cheap smartphone has ended,” said Francisco Jeronimo of IDC. Some smaller brands that rely on cheap phones may not survive, it says.

**PCs.** HP said in February that memory had become about 35% of the cost of the parts in a PC. Dell, HP, Lenovo, Acer and Asus warned customers of price rises of 15–20%. IDC expects PC shipments to fall 11.3% in 2026 and average prices to rise 17%, and says prices are unlikely to return to 2025 levels even after the shortage eases. Apple raised prices on Macs and iPads in June, and its shares had their worst day in over a year.

**Everyone else.** Valve launched its Steam Machine games console in June at $1,049, saying its original price target was “no longer viable”. Graphics card prices rose. Carmakers have lobbied for priority: in April, a US senator asked the Commerce Department to work with the three makers to supply the car industry. Even the makers’ own device businesses are hurt: Samsung’s division that makes phones and TVs is expected to report its second quarterly loss in a row, while its memory division makes a record profit.

<Photo file="Akihabara Electric Town, Tokyo, 20240823 1617 5580.jpg" alt="A busy Tokyo street lined with tall buildings covered in electronics shop signs" caption="Akihabara, Tokyo’s electronics district, photographed in August 2024. In December 2025 some shops there began limiting how much memory each customer could buy, to stop hoarding." />

Consumers are adapting. In South Korea, where Apple raised the price of its cheapest iPhone by 16.1% and Samsung’s mid-range Galaxy A37 costs 19.8% more than its predecessor, more people are repairing broken screens or buying second-hand phones instead of new ones. Kim Sun-woo, an analyst at Meritz Securities, says consumers are starting to treat phones as “durable goods”: things you keep for years, like a fridge, rather than replace every two.

The winners are easy to name. The three big makers are earning margins more often seen in software than in manufacturing.

## Why supply can’t catch up

If memory is so profitable, why don’t the makers simply build more? Because the business has features that make it slow, risky and concentrated, and those features explain most of what is happening.

<WhyList>
<Why title="AI is a different kind of demand">

In past booms, more demand meant more of the same product. This time, the most profitable product, HBM, uses about three times the factory capacity of what it replaces. Even if the makers added wafers at their normal pace, the shift to HBM would leave less ordinary memory. A Samsung executive said in September that he expects HBM to take nearly 30% of the industry’s DRAM wafer capacity in 2027, up from about 20% now. IDC calls this “a potentially permanent, strategic reallocation of the world’s silicon wafer capacity” and “a zero-sum game”.

</Why>
<Why title="New factories take years">

A new memory fab needs a site, power, water, a cleanroom and some of the most expensive machines ever made, and then months to get its output right. SK hynix’s new M15X fab in Cheongju opened its cleanroom in October 2025 and started making chips in early 2026; the first cleanroom at its giant Yongin complex, begun in February 2025, is due to open in early 2027. Micron broke ground on a $9.3 billion expansion in Hiroshima, Japan, in July 2026, aiming for production in late 2028. TrendForce expects meaningful new capacity only from the second half of 2027. When CXMT listed in July, TrendForce’s Ellie Wang noted that capacity expansion “typically takes a year or longer”. Meanwhile, IDC expects DRAM supply to grow only 16% in 2026, well below the norm.

</Why>
<Why title="The industry has been burned before">

Memory is notorious for booms and busts. Because chips are interchangeable, a small surplus sends prices crashing, and every maker is tempted to build when prices are high, which creates the next surplus. Micron says its average selling prices have swung from roughly a 200% increase to a 50% fall within the last five years. In 2023, after the pandemic boom, SK hynix lost 9.1 trillion won in a year; Samsung’s chip business and Micron also made heavy losses, and all three cut production and investment. Japan’s Elpida, once a major DRAM maker, went bankrupt in 2012 after prices fell 85%. <mark>The companies that survived learned to be wary of building too much, too soon, and that caution is now part of why supply is tight.</mark> In March 2026, Korean media reported that Samsung was already preparing for the risk of a downturn.

</Why>
<Why title="Three firms, and no new entrants">

Decades of booms and busts drove most competitors out or into mergers. In 2026 three companies control nearly 88% of DRAM sales. That does not mean they are colluding, and they deny it. But in a market this concentrated, each maker can see that adding capacity aggressively would hurt prices for everyone, including itself. Economists call this tacit coordination: firms behaving similarly without any agreement, because each can predict what the others will do. Such restraint is legal. Agreeing on it is not, and the three have been caught before: between 1998 and 2002, Samsung, Hynix (now SK hynix), Micron and others fixed DRAM prices, and paid hundreds of millions of dollars in US fines.

</Why>
<Why title="The deepest pockets get served first">

When supply is short, sellers choose their customers. The AI buyers are some of the richest companies in history (Meta alone now plans to spend $135–145 billion this year, mostly on data centres), and they are signing multi-year contracts, sometimes with advance payments, to guarantee supply. SK hynix says such contracts cover more than half its capacity for 2027–28. A phone maker selling $100 handsets in Africa or South Asia cannot compete with that. CLSA’s Sanjeev Rana estimates that Samsung and SK hynix can meet only about 60% of their customers’ demand; whoever is at the back of the queue goes short.

</Why>
<Why title="Geopolitics limits the alternative">

The obvious source of extra supply is China. CXMT has grown fast, and Apple, HP and Acer have reportedly tested or used its chips. But since 2019 the US has blocked sales to China of the most advanced chipmaking machines, made by the Dutch firm ASML, which limits how advanced CXMT’s chips can be. CXMT is on a Pentagon list of companies with alleged links to the Chinese military, and in August the US Commerce Secretary, Howard Lutnick, publicly discouraged Apple from buying Chinese memory. Security concerns are real, but they mean that the one competitor growing fast enough to relieve the shortage is one many Western buyers are discouraged from using.

</Why>
</WhyList>

<Answer title="So is it a cartel, a bubble or just the market?">

Mostly, it is the market doing what markets do when a new buyer with enormous resources arrives: scarce factory capacity goes to whoever pays most, and that is now AI. The shortage is real, it was caused by a genuine surge in demand, and new supply genuinely takes years.

But the market’s structure makes the squeeze worse and longer than it would otherwise be. Three companies control the supply, they were badly burned by the last bust, and they have every reason to expand cautiously while prices are high. That is legal, and there is no proof they have colluded; a lawsuit alleging it is at an early stage. Whether this ends in a bust depends on the AI boom itself. If AI spending keeps growing, the makers will eventually build enough; if it slows, today’s new factories could arrive just as demand falls, and the cycle will turn again.

</Answer>

## Is anyone gaming the shortage?

Not everyone accepts that this is just supply and demand. Three challenges stand out.

### The lawsuit

On 25 June 2026, a group of American consumers filed a class action in federal court in California against Samsung, SK hynix and Micron. They allege that the three coordinated a shift of production from older DRAM to HBM to restrict supply and push up prices, which they say rose about 700% over four years. SK hynix disclosed the case in its US listing documents. The companies dispute the claims, and nothing has been proven. A similar suit filed in 2018, after DRAM prices nearly tripled in 2016–17, was dismissed, and an appeals court agreed in 2022 that the plaintiffs had not offered plausible evidence of a conspiracy.

### The customers’ complaints

Some big buyers say the makers are using the shortage to overcharge. In September, Acer’s chief executive, Jason Chen, said he expected memory prices to start falling from late 2027 and accused memory makers of extending their shortage forecasts to keep prices high. In October, Elon Musk, who is planning his own chip factories, posted that “Micron has been reasonable regarding future memory prices” while “others have been extortionate”, without naming them.

Jim Handy of Objective Analysis, a memory research firm, called Musk’s comment hard to understand. “Memory makers do not set their parts’ pricing,” he told The Korea Times; the market does. A maker that priced below its rivals, he said, would simply sell out to its first customers and have nothing left for the rest.

### The forecasts themselves

The makers’ own forecasts of how long the shortage will last have stretched. In March, SK Group’s chairman said it would last until 2030. Micron’s chief executive said in June he expected it to ease in 2028; Samsung says 2028; SK hynix repeated 2030 this week. These forecasts matter because buyers use them to decide whether to sign long-term contracts at today’s prices. Forecasting a long shortage is in a seller’s interest, which is why buyers like Acer are sceptical. It may also simply be accurate.

## The signs of a turn

For all the record profits, there are hints that the peak may be near.

- **Price rises are slowing.** TrendForce expects conventional DRAM prices to rise 10–15% in October–December, after 55–63% in each of the first two quarters. Phone and PC makers are running down existing stocks rather than paying more.
- **Buyers are using less.** Cloud companies and server makers cut the memory in some servers in the first half of 2026, and Nvidia halved the capacity of one type of memory module in its next systems and is considering using fewer HBM layers in a future chip, according to TrendForce. Google announced a technique in March, called TurboQuant, that compresses AI models so they need less memory; memory shares fell on the news.
- **Supply is coming.** New fabs from all three makers start producing in 2027 and 2028, and CXMT is expanding fast, including into HBM.
- **AI spending is under scrutiny.** On 23 October, US tech shares fell sharply after Meta raised its spending plans again; the Nasdaq dropped 2.6%. If the biggest buyers slow their data-centre building, the demand that drives the shortage would slow too.

Handy expects supply not to catch up with demand before 2029 if AI investment keeps growing, but warns that demand could weaken whenever the big data-centre builders slow down, and nobody can know when that will be.

## What this means for policymakers

The memory squeeze is a hard problem for governments, because the obvious levers either do little or have serious side effects. The underlying issue is that an essential input to almost every modern product is made by three companies, mostly in two countries, in an industry whose booms and busts no single government controls.

### The policy problem

- **Prices.** Memory is now a meaningful part of the price of phones, computers, cars and other electronics. Higher device prices feed into inflation, at a time when energy prices are already high because of the war with Iran.
- **Access.** The squeeze falls hardest on the cheapest phones, which are how most people in poorer countries get online. A 20% fall in phone sales in emerging markets means slower progress on digital inclusion, banking and public services delivered by phone.
- **Concentration and resilience.** Three firms, two of them in South Korea, supply nearly 90% of DRAM. Any disruption, from an earthquake to a war on the Korean peninsula, would hit the whole world.
- **Security.** The fastest-growing alternative supplier is Chinese and state-backed, which governments in the US and its allies regard as a security risk.

### The tools, and why each is hard

<Rows>
  <Row label="Competition law">Regulators can investigate whether the makers are coordinating supply or prices, as US and European authorities did successfully in the 2000s. But choosing to expand cautiously is legal, and proving an illegal agreement needs evidence of one. An investigation also takes years, by which time the shortage may be over.</Row>
  <Row label="Subsidies for new fabs">The US CHIPS Act already funds Micron’s fabs in Idaho and New York, and SK hynix’s packaging plant in Indiana; Japan subsidised Micron in Hiroshima; South Korea backs the Yongin cluster. Subsidies can add capacity and spread it across more countries, but they take years to pay off, and adding capacity at the top of a boom risks deepening the next bust, with taxpayers sharing the losses.</Row>
  <Row label="Allowing Chinese supply">Letting companies buy from CXMT would add supply now and lower prices. But it would deepen dependence on a Chinese state-backed firm that the US considers linked to its military, and would help China close the technology gap that export controls are designed to keep open. This is a direct trade-off between cheaper devices and national security.</Row>
  <Row label="Priority and allocation">Governments could press makers to reserve supply for industries they see as essential, such as cars or medical devices, as a US senator suggested in April. But this does not create any more memory; it moves the shortage to someone else, and makers may resist governments directing their customers.</Row>
  <Row label="Tariffs">Tariffs on imported chips, which the US has threatened, would raise prices further for American buyers in the short term, since almost all memory is made abroad. They are meant to encourage domestic production, which takes years.</Row>
  <Row label="Buyers’ own choices">Governments and public bodies are big buyers of computers. They can extend the life of devices, buy refurbished, and encourage repair, which the squeeze is already pushing consumers towards.</Row>
</Rows>

<Answer title="So what can governments actually do?">

In the short term, very little. No government can produce memory faster than the fabs being built, and most of the quick fixes, from forced allocation to tariffs, move the pain around rather than reduce it. The real choices are longer-term and involve trade-offs: whether to keep paying to spread memory production across more countries, knowing that subsidies at the top of a boom can fuel the next bust; how tightly to restrict Chinese suppliers when they are the fastest way to more supply; and whether competition authorities should look harder at an industry where three firms decide how much of a vital input the world gets. For poorer countries that make no chips at all, the main lever is buying smarter and keeping devices longer.

</Answer>

## Where reasonable people disagree

<Debate topic="On whether the memory makers are exploiting the shortage">
  <Side title="They are profiting from scarcity they chose">Three companies control almost all DRAM supply and are earning margins of 75–80%, more than most software companies. They decided to move capacity to HBM and to exit older products, which created the shortage of ordinary memory, and they are expanding cautiously because high prices are good for them. Their forecasts of shortages lasting until 2030 encourage buyers to sign long contracts at today’s prices. Two of them, with others, were caught fixing DRAM prices two decades ago. Customers such as Acer’s chief executive and Elon Musk say they are being overcharged, and a class action argues the shift was coordinated. Whatever the legal answer, consumers in poorer countries are paying for a boom that mainly benefits shareholders and AI companies.</Side>
  <Side title="This is how markets ration scarce goods">The makers did not create the AI boom; they responded to it, as businesses should, by making what customers want most. Prices are set by buyers bidding for limited supply, not by the makers: as analyst Jim Handy notes, a maker that undercut its rivals would simply sell out to its first customers. High prices are the signal that tells the industry to build, and all three are building, but fabs take years. After losing tens of billions in 2023, caution is prudent, not sinister. A lawsuit alleging coordination is unproven, and a similar case in 2018 failed. And today’s profits pay for the next generation of factories that will end the shortage.</Side>
</Debate>

<Debate topic="On whether the shortage will end in a bust">
  <Side title="This time is structural">AI is not a passing fad but a lasting change in what computers do, and its memory needs are growing with every new generation of chips and models. HBM permanently uses more wafers per gigabyte, so even expanding capacity leaves less ordinary memory. Long-term contracts with the biggest cloud companies cover much of the makers’ output into 2028. The companies with the best view of their order books, SK hynix and Samsung, both say the tightness lasts until 2028 or 2030. Memory may have become, like oil, a strategic input whose price stays high.</Side>
  <Side title="Every memory boom ends the same way">Memory makers have said “this time is different” before. Prices are already rising more slowly, buyers are trimming the memory in their products, new compression techniques are reducing memory needs, and a wave of new fabs, plus a fast-growing Chinese competitor, will arrive in 2027–28. If investors’ doubts about AI spending turn into cuts by the big cloud companies, that new supply will meet falling demand. Several of the long-term contracts reportedly include price ceilings. The memory industry’s history suggests the higher prices go now, the harder they will fall.</Side>
</Debate>

## Further context: memory’s boom-and-bust history

Memory chips were among the first products of the silicon age. Intel was founded in 1968 largely to make them, but was driven out of DRAM in the mid-1980s by Japanese makers such as NEC, Hitachi and Toshiba, who in turn were overtaken in the 1990s by South Korea’s Samsung and Hyundai’s chip division, which became Hynix and then SK hynix. Each shift came through the same process: heavy investment during busts, when rivals cut back, so as to have the most capacity when demand returned.

The industry’s history is a series of brutal cycles. Prices collapse whenever supply runs ahead of demand, as it did in 2001, 2008–09, 2011–12, 2019 and 2022–23. Losers went bankrupt or were bought: Germany’s Qimonda in 2009, Japan’s Elpida in 2012, which Micron bought the following year. By the mid-2010s, three companies were left making most of the world’s DRAM, and the industry hoped that consolidation would end the wild swings. The 2023 bust, and today’s shortage, suggest it has not; it has made the swings more profitable for the survivors.

China has spent the past decade trying to break into the industry, through state-backed companies such as CXMT for DRAM and YMTC for flash storage. The US has tried to slow it with export controls on chipmaking equipment. CXMT’s rise during this shortage shows how a period of high prices gives a newcomer the opening it needs.

## Key terms

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="DRAM">Dynamic random-access memory: the fast, temporary working memory in phones, computers and servers.</Term>
  <Term name="NAND flash">The type of memory used to store data long-term, in SSDs, phones and memory cards.</Term>
  <Term name="High-bandwidth memory (HBM)">Stacked DRAM chips placed right next to an AI processor to feed it data very fast. Expensive and wafer-hungry to make.</Term>
  <Term name="Wafer">A thin disc of silicon on which chips are made. Factory capacity is measured in wafers per month.</Term>
  <Term name="Fab">A semiconductor factory. A modern memory fab costs tens of billions of dollars and takes years to build.</Term>
  <Term name="Contract price">The price agreed between memory makers and large buyers, usually each quarter. The main benchmark for the industry.</Term>
  <Term name="Long-term agreement (LTA)">A multi-year supply contract, often with guaranteed volumes and sometimes price bands or advance payments.</Term>
  <Term name="Hyperscaler">The largest cloud computing companies, such as Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta, which build data centres on a huge scale.</Term>
  <Term name="Bill of materials">The total cost of the parts that go into a product, before assembly, marketing and profit.</Term>
  <Term name="Export controls">Government rules restricting the sale of certain goods or technology abroad, used by the US to limit China’s access to advanced chipmaking tools.</Term>
</KeyTerms>

### Concepts worth learning properly

<Concepts>
  <Concept title="Oligopoly and tacit coordination">How a market with a few big sellers behaves, and why firms can act alike without ever agreeing to. Central to the debate over whether the makers are holding back supply.</Concept>
  <Concept title="The cobweb model (the hog cycle)">Why industries where new supply takes years to arrive swing between shortage and glut. Memory is a textbook case.</Concept>
  <Concept title="Opportunity cost">The value of what you give up when you use a resource one way. Every wafer turned into HBM is ordinary memory not made.</Concept>
  <Concept title="Price as a rationing mechanism">How prices decide who gets a scarce good when supply can’t grow. Here, it means the richest buyers are served first.</Concept>
</Concepts>
