<Overview>

On 25 September 2026, Singapore’s government confirmed what demographers had predicted for years: the country is now a “super-aged” society. Its annual *Population in Brief* report showed that 21.4% of citizens are 65 or older, above the 21% line the United Nations uses for the label. A decade ago the figure was 13.7%. The number of citizens aged 80 and over has risen by 60% in ten years, to 152,000, and by 2035 more than one in four citizens will be 65 or older.

At the same time, fewer babies are being born than ever. Singapore’s resident total fertility rate fell to 0.87 children per woman in 2025, among the lowest in the world and far below the 2.1 needed for a population to replace itself. Citizen births fell by more than a tenth in a single year. So the population is growing older from both ends: people are living longer, and fewer young people are arriving to replace the old. In 2016 there were 4.7 citizens of working age for every citizen aged 65 and over. Today there are 2.8. By 2035 there will be 2.2.

This matters more in Singapore than in most rich countries because of how the state is built. Singapore has no state pension and keeps public spending low. Instead, people are expected to pay for their own old age and health care from compulsory savings accounts, backed by family and, increasingly, by targeted government help. The country spends less than 5% of its economy on health care, roughly half of what other developed countries spend. This issue explains how that model works, where ageing is putting it under strain (in hospitals, in care homes and families, at work and in retirement), and the question underneath it all: can a low-cost, self-reliant system survive a population this old, and if not, who pays the difference?

</Overview>

## What “super-aged” actually means

<Sidenote term="Aged and super-aged">Labels used by the UN and demographers. A society is “ageing” when 7% of people are 65 or older, “aged” at 14% and “super-aged” at 21%.</Sidenote>

The label itself is just a threshold: a super-aged society is one where at least 21% of people are 65 or older. Japan crossed it around 2007 and is now close to 30%. Italy, Germany and several other European countries are past it. South Korea’s statistics ministry reported this month that 21.6% of its whole population is now 65 or older.

Two details about Singapore’s figure are worth knowing. First, Singapore counts **citizens only**, while the UN uses the whole population. Singapore has about 3.68 million citizens, but its total population is 6.21 million, including 0.55 million permanent residents and almost 2 million foreigners living there on work passes and other visas. Most foreign workers are of working age, so counting everyone would make the country look younger: among all residents (citizens plus permanent residents), about 19.5% are 65 or older. The citizen figure is the more honest picture for public policy, because citizens are the people who draw on subsidies and will retire in Singapore.

Second, what makes Singapore unusual is not how old it is, but <mark>how fast it got old</mark>. It took France more than a century to go from 7% to 14% older people. Singapore did it in 19 years, and then took only about nine more to reach 21%. Japan, often seen as the model of a fast-ageing country, took 24 years to go from 7% to 14%. Speed matters because institutions take decades to adapt: pension savings need a working lifetime to build up, care homes and nurses take years to train and build, and voters’ expectations change slowly.

### Why the support ratio matters

<Sidenote term="Old-age support ratio">The number of people of working age for each older person. Singapore’s official version counts citizens aged 20–64 for every citizen aged 65 and over. Other sources use residents or ages 15–64, which gives different numbers.</Sidenote>

The single most important number in the report is the old-age support ratio: 2.8 working-age citizens for every older citizen, down from 4.7 in 2016 and heading for 2.2 by 2035. It is a rough measure of how many people are earning, paying taxes and available to care for each older person.

The ratio does not mean each worker literally supports 0.36 of a pensioner. Many people over 65 keep working, many under 65 don’t, and in Singapore older people mostly pay for themselves from savings. But the ratio does capture three real pressures. There are fewer taxpayers for each person drawing on public health care. There are fewer adult children for each frail parent. And there are fewer workers in the economy overall, which slows growth unless productivity or immigration make up the difference.

## The Singapore model: pay your own way

To understand why ageing is a particular test for Singapore, you need to understand how it pays for old age. Most rich countries use some version of a **pay-as-you-go** system: today’s workers pay taxes or social insurance contributions, and that money funds today’s pensioners and today’s health care. When there are fewer workers for each pensioner, the system strains, and governments have to raise taxes, cut benefits or borrow.

<Sidenote term="Pay-as-you-go">A pension or health system where current workers’ contributions pay current retirees’ benefits, rather than each person saving for themselves. Most European state pensions work this way.</Sidenote>

Singapore chose a different path. At its centre is the **Central Provident Fund (CPF)**, a compulsory savings scheme started under British rule in 1955. Workers up to 55 put 20% of their wages into personal CPF accounts, and employers add up to 17%. The money is the worker’s own, earns guaranteed interest, and is split between accounts for housing, health care and retirement. At 55, members set aside a retirement sum (for people turning 55 in 2026, the Basic Retirement Sum is S$110,200 and the Full Retirement Sum S$220,400), which from 65 pays a monthly income for life through an annuity called CPF LIFE. Someone with the full sum can expect roughly S$1,700–1,800 a month.

Health care follows the same logic. The government calls it **“S+3Ms”**:

<Rows>
  <Row label="Subsidies">The government pays a large share of the bill in public hospitals and clinics, more for poorer patients and cheaper wards. About half of the national health bill is paid from tax revenue.</Row>
  <Row label="MediSave">A compulsory medical savings account inside the CPF. People use their own savings to pay hospital bills and insurance premiums.</Row>
  <Row label="MediShield Life">Compulsory, lifelong public insurance against large hospital bills, covering everyone including people with existing illnesses. From June 2026 it pays up to S$200,000 a year, with deductibles and co-payments.</Row>
  <Row label="MediFund">A government endowment fund that is the safety net for patients who still cannot pay after everything else.</Row>
</Rows>

The design has two goals. One is to keep costs down. Because patients always pay something themselves (a **co-payment**), they and their doctors have a reason not to overuse care. The other is to avoid the problem pay-as-you-go systems face as they age: if everyone saves for themselves, the shrinking number of young people doesn’t directly matter. Health Minister Ong Ye Kung credited the co-payment design in March 2026 for keeping health spending below 5% of GDP while life expectancy is among the highest in the world.

<Sidenote term="Co-payment">The share of a bill the patient pays even when insurance or subsidies cover the rest. Designed to discourage unnecessary use.</Sidenote>

The model also leans heavily on two other things that are rarely written into law: **families** and **cheap labour**. Children are expected to help their parents (Singapore even has a law, the Maintenance of Parents Act, that lets parents sue children for support). And hundreds of thousands of households hire live-in domestic workers from Indonesia, the Philippines and Myanmar, many of whom end up caring for older people.

For decades this worked well, in part because Singapore was young. Ageing tests every piece of it.

## Where ageing bites: hospitals and health costs

Older people use far more health care than younger people. They have more chronic illnesses such as diabetes, heart disease and dementia, they are admitted to hospital more often and they stay longer. So as the population ages, health spending rises even if prices and treatments stay the same.

The numbers are moving fast. The government’s health budget for 2026 is about S$22.5 billion, its second-largest item after defence, and about 10% more than the year before. Ong told Parliament in 2025 that government health spending would rise from about S$21 billion to more than S$30 billion by 2030. In March 2026 he put it in terms of the economy: government health spending is about 2.7% of GDP this year and will rise to around 3.5% by 2030. That is a large jump for a government that tries to balance its budget. A Ministry of Finance paper in 2023 gave a similar estimate and named three causes: an older population, more use of health care at every age, and higher medical costs.

### People are living longer, but not all of it healthily

One reason costs keep rising is that extra years of life are not all healthy years. In September 2026, the Ministry of Health told Parliament that life expectancy in Singapore was 85.4 years in 2023 (based on the Global Burden of Disease study), but healthy life expectancy was only 74.1. The gap, roughly the years spent in poor health at the end of life, has grown from 10.6 years in 2014 to 11.2 years. That is still better than the OECD average of 12.6, but it is growing, and those are the most expensive years.

<Sidenote term="Healthy life expectancy">The average number of years a person can expect to live in good health, without serious illness or disability. Also called health-adjusted life expectancy (HALE).</Sidenote>

### Who feels the cost

For patients, the main pressure points are insurance premiums and what they pay themselves. MediShield Life premiums rise steeply with age: the government’s table runs from S$200 a year for young people to S$2,826 a year for people over 90, before subsidies. The current premium table took effect in April 2025, and claim limits were raised in June 2026 to keep up with bigger hospital bills. Most people pay premiums from MediSave, so the cost is felt as a draining savings account rather than cash out of pocket, until the account runs low.

Singapore also has one of the highest shares of **out-of-pocket** spending among rich countries. An international profile put it at about 30% of health spending in 2019, well above the share in most other rich countries. That is by design, but it means an older person with a long illness, or a family supporting one, bears more of the cost directly than in most rich countries. Critics such as academics at a National University of Singapore forum have also argued that the 3Ms cover only a modest share of total health spending and that means-testing, in which patients prove their income to get subsidies, adds stress for the sick and their families.

## Where ageing bites: care, families and domestic workers

Hospitals are only part of the story. The harder problem, in Singapore as in every ageing country, is **long-term care**: the daily help that frail or disabled older people need with washing, dressing, eating and moving around, often for years. Long-term care is not mainly medical, so it falls outside most health insurance. It is labour-intensive, so it is hard to make cheaper with technology. And historically, most of it has been done for free by women in the family.

<Sidenote term="Long-term care">Ongoing help with daily living for people who can’t manage alone because of age, illness or disability, given at home, in day centres or in nursing homes.</Sidenote>

Singapore’s demand for it is climbing quickly. By 2040, one in three older people will be 80 or over, according to Senior Minister of State Tan Kiat How. The number of people aged 65 and over living alone more than doubled between 2014 and 2024, to 87,200. The government’s spending on long-term care has nearly doubled in five years to about S$3 billion a year, according to Ong in 2025.

### Nursing homes and care at home

The government has been building fast. It had about 16,000 nursing home beds in 2020 and aims for about 31,000 by 2030, including 10,000 more beds over the five years to 2030. But in a September 2026 answer to Parliament, the Ministry of Health said land and manpower limits mean it cannot meet rising demand by adding beds alone. Its strategy is to help people **age in place**: stay in their own homes as long as possible, with help from day centres, home nursing, community networks and redesigned housing.

That strategy has produced a stream of programmes. Age Well SG, launched in 2023, has expanded a network of more than 200 Active Ageing Centres where older people can exercise, eat and socialise, and in 2026 named four “Age Well Neighbourhoods” serving more than 110,000 older people. Housing agency HDB builds Community Care Apartments that combine small flats with on-site care. And in Budget 2025 the government raised long-term care subsidies to a maximum of 80% from July 2026, and raised the Home Caregiving Grant, a monthly cash payment to families caring for a severely disabled relative, from S$400 to up to S$600 from April 2026.

<Photo file="Man Fut Tong Nursing Home—Singapore.jpg" alt="The building of Man Fut Tong Nursing Home in Singapore" caption="Man Fut Tong Nursing Home in Singapore. The government aims to have about 31,000 nursing home beds by 2030, nearly double the 2020 figure." />

### The families and workers who actually do the work

Most care still happens at home, and it is done by two groups. One is family members, mostly daughters, wives and daughters-in-law, many of whom cut their working hours or leave jobs to care. A Duke-NUS Medical School estimate put the value of unpaid care for people aged 75 and over at about S$1.28 billion a year. In Parliament in March 2026, opposition MP Fadli Fawzi of the Workers’ Party argued Singapore was becoming “a nation of caregivers” whose support was fragmented and who had no formal recognition. A 2024 study by economists for the US National Bureau of Economic Research projected average family eldercare hours rising sharply as the population ages.

The other group is migrant domestic workers. There were about 308,700 in Singapore by mid-2025, up from 247,400 at the end of 2020, according to a March 2026 commentary from the Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy, which called the situation a “care paradox”: a first-world economy relying on low-paid care labour. Households with a citizen aged 67 or older pay a reduced government levy of S$60 a month for a domestic worker instead of S$300. That concession is effectively a public subsidy for private, live-in eldercare.

<mark>Domestic workers are the hidden pillar of Singapore’s low-cost care model.</mark> They make it possible for many families to keep a frail parent at home without paying for professional care. But researchers have warned that the arrangement may not last. Workers often have little training in caring for people with dementia or disabilities. They earn far less than local care workers would, and as wages rise in Indonesia and the Philippines, and as Japan, Taiwan, Hong Kong and the Gulf compete for the same workers, they are likely to become more expensive and harder to recruit.

### Paying for care: CareShield Life

To help families pay, the government introduced **CareShield Life** in 2020, compulsory insurance that pays a monthly cash amount for life to anyone who becomes severely disabled (unable to do at least three of six basic daily activities). It covers people born from 1980; older cohorts are covered by an older, smaller scheme called ElderShield, with optional switching. In 2025 the government accepted recommendations to increase payouts by 4% a year until 2030, and set aside more than S$570 million to soften the premium increases that came with them. Payouts are in the hundreds of dollars a month, enough to help with costs, not to pay for full-time professional care.

## Where ageing bites: work and retirement income

If people live longer, one obvious answer is to work longer. Singapore has been moving in that direction steadily. Its law does not let employers force workers to retire before a minimum retirement age, and requires them to offer re-employment, usually on a yearly contract, to eligible workers up to a higher re-employment age. On 1 July 2026 these rose to 64 and 69. Both are due to rise to 65 and 70 by 2030. A Tripartite Workgroup on Senior Employment, made up of the government, the national trade union congress (NTUC) and the main employers’ federation (SNEF), has been studying ideas such as part-time and phased retirement and is due to report in the second half of 2026.

<Sidenote term="Tripartism">Singapore’s practice of making labour policy jointly between the government, the NTUC union federation and employers. It is credited with labour peace but critics say it limits independent worker voice.</Sidenote>

Older Singaporeans already work far more than their peers in most rich countries. About 31% of residents aged 65 and over were employed in 2022, a rate the manpower minister called high by OECD standards. But overall labour force participation is falling slowly because the population is ageing: it dropped to 67.9% in 2025, the fourth fall in a row. And many older workers are in low-paid jobs such as cleaning, security and food service. For some, working into their seventies is a choice; for others it is a necessity.

### Is retirement income enough?

The CPF system has improved. In 2020 about 66% of active CPF members turning 55 had saved the Basic Retirement Sum; by 2022 it was almost 70%, and the government expects about 80% by 2027. That still leaves a large minority, mostly lower-income workers and people (often women) who spent years out of paid work caring for children or parents, with small savings and small monthly payouts. Someone retiring on the Basic Retirement Sum can expect roughly S$950 a month from CPF LIFE.

For them, the state has added layers of targeted help over the last decade:

<Rows>
  <Row label="Silver Support">Quarterly cash for older people with low lifetime earnings and little family support. About 290,000 people aged 65 and over receive it. Payments rose 20% in 2025 and the income limit was raised.</Row>
  <Row label="Matching schemes">The government matches top-ups to the CPF retirement and MediSave accounts of older people with low balances, including a new Matched MediSave Scheme from 2026 to 2030.</Row>
  <Row label="Generation packages">Lifelong extra health subsidies for the Pioneer (born before 1950) and Merdeka (born in the 1950s) generations.</Row>
  <Row label="One-off bonuses">The 2025 Majulah Package and similar one-off top-ups to CPF accounts.</Row>
</Rows>

How much is enough? A 2019 study led by Ng Kok Hoe of the Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy asked ordinary Singaporeans to define a basic standard of living and priced it. It found a single person over 65 needed about S$1,379 a month, which would be higher today with inflation. A 2023 Singapore Management University survey found nearly one in three older adults thought there was at least an even chance they would be unable to afford basic necessities. The government disputes the framing that a large share of older Singaporeans are poor, pointing out that many own their flats outright and receive transfers, but the gap between CPF LIFE payouts at the basic level and these estimates is real.

## Immigration: the buffer, and its limits

The one lever that directly changes the shape of the population is immigration. Singapore granted 25,094 citizenships and 35,352 permanent residencies in 2025. Deputy Prime Minister Gan Kim Yong said in September that Singapore expects to take in 25,000 to 30,000 new citizens a year over the next five years, and about 40,000 new permanent residents a year. *Population in Brief 2026* says immigration is one reason the share of citizens of working age should stay broadly stable between 2030 and 2035.

On top of that, foreign workers fill jobs Singaporeans don’t want or can’t fill, including most care work. The non-resident population grew 3.7% in the year to June 2026, to 1.98 million, and was the main reason the total population rose to 6.21 million.

But immigration is politically sensitive. The 2013 Population White Paper, which suggested a population of up to 6.9 million by 2030, set off protests that drew an estimated 3,000 to 5,000 people to Hong Lim Park, one of the largest demonstrations in decades. Since then the government has tightened work-pass rules and talked more cautiously about numbers, while keeping intakes steady. It also manages immigration to keep the citizen population’s ethnic balance stable, which shapes who it admits.

Immigration also cannot solve ageing on its own. Immigrants grow old too, and to keep the support ratio stable as fertility falls would need ever-larger intakes. What it can do is slow the decline and fill specific gaps, including in the care workforce.

## Why ageing strains Singapore’s model so much

Every rich country is ageing. Why does it pose a particular question for Singapore? It is tempting to say the model is simply too stingy, or that the government failed to plan. The truth is more layered: some of it is the model’s design, some is demography, and some is a political choice about who should carry risk.

<WhyList>
<Why title="Ageing at record speed leaves no time to adjust">

Singapore went from young to super-aged in about 28 years. Many of today’s older citizens started working when wages were low and CPF contribution rates were lower, so they had less time to build savings. A self-funded system works best when each generation saves through a full working life at decent wages. The first generations of a fast-ageing society don’t get that, which is why the government has had to create special packages for the Pioneer and Merdeka generations.

</Why>
<Why title="Fertility has collapsed and incentives haven’t reversed it">

The resident fertility rate has been below replacement since the late 1970s and fell to 0.87 in 2025. Decades of baby bonuses, housing priority and tax breaks have not reversed it. The reasons are similar to those across East Asia: expensive housing and education, long working hours, intense competition in schooling, the high cost (in money and careers) of children for women, and later and fewer marriages. Fewer children means fewer future workers and fewer adult children to care for parents.

</Why>
<Why title="Self-reliance pools less risk">

Individual savings accounts are good at one thing, making people save, and weak at another: sharing risk. Some people live to 100; some need ten years of care; some get cancer at 60. A system that relies mostly on your own account leaves those people more exposed than a system that pools risk across everyone. Singapore has slowly added more pooling (MediShield Life in 2015, CareShield Life in 2020, CPF LIFE’s annuity), which is a quiet admission that pure self-reliance doesn’t cover the costliest risks of old age.

</Why>
<Why title="Care was handed to families and cheap labour">

Long-term care was kept cheap for the state by relying on unpaid family members, mostly women, and on low-paid migrant domestic workers. That is efficient in the short run but becomes harder as families get smaller, more women work, more older people live alone, and foreign workers get more expensive. These costs never appear in the health budget, so official spending figures understate what care really costs society.

</Why>
<Why title="Fiscal rules limit how fast the state can spend">

Singapore’s constitution requires the government to balance its budget over each term and protects past reserves, which can be drawn down only with the elected President’s approval. The government can spend up to half of the expected long-term returns on its reserves each year (the Net Investment Returns Contribution), which already funds about one-fifth of the budget. It is a rich state with large savings, but its rules are designed to stop today’s politicians from spending them. Higher spending on ageing therefore means higher taxes, cuts elsewhere or a change in how much of the investment returns can be spent.

</Why>
<Why title="Low spending is partly a political choice">

The governing People’s Action Party has long argued that generous welfare erodes the work ethic and leads to the high taxes and debt seen in Europe. That belief has kept taxes low and targeted help at those who need it most. Supporters say it is why Singapore has strong public finances. Critics say it means the state underspends on older people and leaves individuals and families with risks that are better shared. Either way, the size of the state is a decision, not a law of nature.

</Why>
</WhyList>

<Answer title="So can a low-cost, self-reliant model survive ageing?">

Partly, and only by becoming less low-cost and less self-reliant than it used to be. The model has already changed a lot: compulsory lifelong insurance for hospital bills and disability, lifelong annuities, cash support for poor older people and steadily rising subsidies. Health spending is rising towards levels that are still modest by rich-country standards but large for Singapore. The government has also raised GST, in part to pay for this.

**Who pays?** For now, the cost is spread across four groups: **older people themselves**, through their savings, premiums, co-payments and working longer; **families**, through unpaid care and money; **migrant workers**, through low wages for care work; and increasingly **taxpayers**, through GST and the returns on national reserves. The big policy question for the next decade is how much of the cost shifts from the first three groups to the last, and how fast.

</Answer>

## What this means for Singapore’s government

The policy problem is straightforward to state and hard to solve. Spending on health and care must rise for at least the next two decades, while the share of the population of working age falls. The government wants to keep taxes low, budgets balanced, reserves protected and individuals responsible for much of their own costs. It cannot keep all of those promises at once indefinitely. Here are the main tools, and why each one is hard to use.

<Rows>
  <Row label="Raise taxes">GST went from 7% to 9% in 2023–2024 and was expected to bring in about 0.7% of GDP a year. A further rise would hit poorer households hardest unless offset by rebates, and GST hikes have been politically costly. Taxes on wealth and property are the alternative the opposition favours; they raise less and risk deterring the wealthy residents Singapore courts.</Row>
  <Row label="Spend more of the reserves">The government could raise the share of investment returns it spends. This is the least painful option today, but it reduces what is left for future generations, who will be even older, and needs constitutional changes or the President’s agreement for past reserves.</Row>
  <Row label="Ask individuals to pay more">Higher premiums, co-payments or CPF contribution rates keep the cost off the budget but fall on older people and low earners, who can least afford it, and are politically unpopular.</Row>
  <Row label="Work longer">Raising the retirement and re-employment ages increases incomes, savings and tax revenue. It depends on employers wanting older workers, on workers being healthy enough to work, and it does little for people in physically demanding jobs.</Row>
  <Row label="Prevent illness">Healthier SG, launched in 2023, enrols people with a family doctor and pays for screening and preventive care, aiming to shrink the years spent in poor health. Prevention can improve lives, but the evidence that it reduces total spending in an ageing population is mixed; people who live longer still need care eventually.</Row>
  <Row label="Bring in people">More immigrants and foreign workers ease labour shortages and slow the fall in the support ratio. They also raise housing and crowding pressures and provoke political backlash, as in 2013.</Row>
  <Row label="Build and redesign care">More nursing homes, day centres and home care, plus better pay and training for local care workers. This takes land, money and years, and the care workforce is hard to recruit for in a tight labour market.</Row>
</Rows>

Some constraints are particular to Singapore. Land is scarce, which is why the health ministry says it cannot meet care demand just by building more beds. Its labour market is tight: unemployment is low and the health and care sectors compete with every other industry for workers. And its key care labour supply, migrant domestic workers, depends on wages and rules in other countries, especially Indonesia, the Philippines and Myanmar, and on competition from other places that hire the same workers.

What is actually on the table now is incremental: steadily higher health budgets, retirement ages reaching 65 and 70 by 2030, the forthcoming senior employment workgroup report, rising CareShield Life payouts, more nursing home beds and an emphasis on prevention and ageing at home. The government has said repeatedly that more revenue will be needed this decade, but it has not said what form it will take. Its approach so far is to make changes in small, pre-announced steps rather than a single overhaul, which avoids shocks but can also mean problems are addressed late.

## Where reasonable people disagree

<Debate topic="On how much the state should pay">
  <Side title="Keep self-reliance at the core">Singapore’s model has delivered long lives at half the cost of other rich countries, with strong public finances and no national debt crisis. European-style systems have created huge unfunded promises that younger generations will struggle to pay. Targeted help for those who need it, plus savings, insurance and working longer, is fairer to the young and more sustainable. Every dollar of extra spending must come from taxes on a shrinking workforce.</Side>
  <Side title="Share the risks more widely">The costliest risks of old age (long illness, dementia, years of care) are exactly the ones individuals can’t save for, and they fall hardest on low earners and on women who gave up work to care. Singapore is rich, with large reserves and low taxes, and can afford to pool more of these risks. Low official spending partly reflects costs pushed onto families and migrant workers, not real efficiency.</Side>
</Debate>

<Debate topic="On immigration as an answer to ageing">
  <Side title="Immigration is essential">With fertility at 0.87, nothing else can stop the workforce shrinking quickly. Immigrants and foreign workers keep the economy growing, pay taxes, and do care work locals won’t. Restricting them would make ageing worse and care more expensive.</Side>
  <Side title="Immigration is a short-term fix">Immigrants also age, and relying on them postpones hard choices about productivity, wages for care workers and family policy. Rapid population growth puts pressure on housing, transport and social cohesion. The better path is to raise productivity, pay care workers properly and make it easier for Singaporeans to have the families they say they want.</Side>
</Debate>

## Further context: why Singapore is wary of welfare

Singapore’s founders came to power in the 1950s and 1960s watching Britain build a welfare state, and drew the opposite lesson. Lee Kuan Yew often argued that state benefits sap the will to work and that Singapore, with no natural resources, could not afford them. The CPF, inherited from colonial rule, became the vehicle for a different idea: the state would make people save, help them buy a home, and step in only as a last resort. Home ownership, now about 90%, was meant to give every family a stake in the country and an asset to fall back on in old age. That philosophy has softened, especially since a 2011 election in which the governing party lost ground and turned more attention to the poor and the old. But its core belief, that help should be targeted and earned, still shapes how Singapore is responding to ageing.

## Key terms

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="Super-aged society">A society where at least 21% of people are 65 or older.</Term>
  <Term name="Old-age support ratio">Number of working-age people for each older person; in Singapore’s official measure, citizens aged 20–64 per citizen aged 65+.</Term>
  <Term name="Total fertility rate">The average number of children a woman would have over her life at current birth rates. 2.1 keeps a population stable.</Term>
  <Term name="CPF">Central Provident Fund, Singapore’s compulsory savings scheme for retirement, housing and health care.</Term>
  <Term name="CPF LIFE">The annuity that turns CPF retirement savings into a monthly income for life from age 65.</Term>
  <Term name="S+3Ms">Singapore’s health financing model: Subsidies, MediSave, MediShield Life and MediFund.</Term>
  <Term name="CareShield Life">Compulsory insurance paying monthly cash to people who become severely disabled.</Term>
  <Term name="Re-employment age">The age up to which employers must offer eligible older workers a new contract.</Term>
  <Term name="Ageing in place">Helping older people stay in their own homes and communities rather than moving into institutions.</Term>
  <Term name="NIRC">Net Investment Returns Contribution: the share of returns on Singapore’s reserves that the government can spend each year.</Term>
</KeyTerms>
