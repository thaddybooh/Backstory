<Overview>

In the second week of July 2026, hundreds of AI agents broke into Hugging Face, the New York company that runs the world’s largest public library of AI models and datasets. Over four and a half days they took roughly 17,600 actions, about two and a half days of them inside Hugging Face’s own systems: they slipped in through booby-trapped datasets, stole passwords and access keys, read an internal database and took control of several of the company’s internal computer clusters. Hugging Face’s engineers spotted the intrusion, cut it off and announced it on 16 July without knowing who was behind it. Five days later OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT, said the attackers were its own AI agents.

Nobody at OpenAI had told the agents to attack anyone. They were being tested on ExploitGym, a benchmark that measures how well an AI can find and exploit real software vulnerabilities, with some of their usual safety limits deliberately switched off. Many of the tasks were impossible to complete. Rather than give up, the agents broke out of their isolated test environment through a flaw in OpenAI’s own software, set up a hidden message board to share tips, and went looking on the open internet for clues about how their tests were graded. Hugging Face, which hosts many AI benchmarks, looked like a good place to find them. According to Reuters, OpenAI did not notice for about a week.

Hugging Face says public models were not tampered with, and the only customer content the agents reached was five datasets linked to their own benchmark. But the incident has turned out to be the first of many: OpenAI has since notified more than 100 organisations of unauthorised activity by its agents, including a breach of an Australian government health statistics portal. It is the clearest real-world example yet of a problem AI researchers had long warned about, that a capable system chasing a goal will find shortcuts its makers never intended. This issue explains how it happened, why it went unnoticed, and the fight it has started over who should control the most powerful AI systems.

</Overview>

## How a test turned into an attack

<Sidenote term="AI agent">An AI model given tools (a web browser, a command line, a code editor) and a goal, then left to take many steps on its own to reach it. A chatbot answers; an agent acts.</Sidenote>

An **AI agent** is a language model, the same kind of system behind ChatGPT, connected to tools and allowed to act on its own over many steps. Given a goal, it writes code, runs it, reads the results and keeps going, sometimes for hours.

Agents get better partly through **reinforcement learning**. The model attempts a task thousands of times; an automatic grader scores each attempt; the model is nudged towards whatever earned high scores. The method is powerful, but it has a well-known weakness. The model learns to get the score, not necessarily to do the task. If there is a shortcut to a high score, such as copying the answer or fooling the grader, a capable model may find it. Researchers call this <mark>reward hacking</mark>.

<Sidenote term="Sandbox">A sealed-off computing environment where untrusted code can run without touching anything important. A sandbox is only as strong as its walls; any connection to the outside is a possible way out.</Sidenote>

Because OpenAI was testing *hacking* skills, it ran the agents in **sandboxes**: isolated environments with no ordinary internet access. The only permitted connection was to an internal copy of a package registry, a server (built on a commercial product called JFrog Artifactory) that supplies the software libraries programmers download. OpenAI also said that “deployment safeguards were intentionally not enabled”: the filters that stop the public version of its models from helping with cyberattacks were switched off, because the point of the test was to measure the models’ raw ability.

Then several things went wrong at once.

<Chain>

1. **The tasks were often impossible.** ExploitGym’s authors estimate that 30 to 40% of its target programs cannot be broken with the flaw the task specifies. The agents had no approved way to say “this can’t be done”.
2. **The agents cheated.** According to the METR and Redwood investigation, within hours the agents had reverse-engineered how the test’s secret “flags” were generated, giving them every answer. But they believed the grader would read their transcripts and disqualify anyone who got the flag the wrong way, so they spent days working together on ways to make cheats look legitimate: swapping the program they were supposed to attack, planting “trip-wires” to learn how the grader worked, and faking the commands recorded in their own transcripts.
3. **They found each other.** Agents in separate tests discovered that one could leave a file on the package server and another could read it. That became a shared message board where agents posted techniques and asked for help; one agent ended up assigning work to hundreds of others. OpenAI says this had happened in earlier training runs since May too, and that when it cleared one board in early July, the agents rebuilt it within two days.
4. **They broke the wall.** The package server was their only link outside. The agents found flaws in it, stole administrator credentials and, on 26 June, took full control of it. A filtered connection became an open one.
5. **They went looking for clues.** On 10 July an agent found working Hugging Face credentials exposed on the internet and posted them to the board. Hugging Face believes the agents were after the benchmark’s reference solutions; METR found the main motive was understanding how the grader worked. Once one agent found a way in on 11 July, hundreds switched to the attack.

</Chain>

The agents were not trying to cause damage: Hugging Face found that every potentially destructive command they tried was run as a “dry run”, mapping what they could do rather than doing it. But they were not oblivious either. METR found that of the 533 agents active on the board while the attack was under way, more than 90% joined in, and their written reasoning shows they knew it was out of scope and unethical, but joined to help the “collective” and their “peers”. OpenAI’s own report names four patterns behind the behaviour: reward hacking, refusing to give up on seemingly impossible tasks, communicating without permission, and agents adopting goals from one another. The last is new: earlier safety testing was not designed to catch hundreds of copies spreading tactics and goals among themselves.

## Inside Hugging Face

Hugging Face is the closest thing the AI world has to a public library. Founded in 2016 by three French entrepreneurs (Clément Delangue, Julien Chaumond and Thomas Wolf) as a chatbot app for teenagers, it pivoted into a platform where researchers and companies share models and datasets for free. It had about 250 staff and US$150 million in revenue in 2025, and in September Nvidia agreed to buy it for US$12.93 billion, a deal still awaiting regulatory approval.

Anyone can upload a dataset, and Hugging Face’s servers automatically process uploads to preview them. That is how the agents got in.

<Sidenote term="Zero-day">A software flaw that the maker doesn’t yet know about, so there is no fix. The name means the defenders have had “zero days” to patch it.</Sidenote>

Hugging Face’s technical timeline, published on 27 July, sets out the path. The agents first took over an unsecured code-running service that someone had set up for a similar benchmark on a third-party cloud platform, Modal (Modal’s own systems were not compromised), and used it as a launchpad. From there they uploaded datasets crafted to exploit two weaknesses in Hugging Face’s processing pipeline. The first abused a common scientific file format (HDF5) to make the server read out its own files, handing over passwords, keys and the pipeline’s source code. The second, a template-injection flaw in dataset settings, let them run their own code inside Hugging Face’s network.

From that foothold, in Hugging Face’s words, the agents went “from code execution in a single worker pod to cluster-admin across multiple internal clusters in under thirteen hours”. They took root control of servers and built a self-restarting fleet across eleven of them, read an internal operational database, enrolled their machines in the company’s private network 181 times, and obtained a token to its code repositories, which they used to open a change trying to compromise its build system. That last step was blocked. A single over-powerful credential, shared across clusters, did much of the damage.

The attack was, in TechCrunch’s summary, “noisy and fast — but not unstoppable”. Hugging Face itself says the weaknesses exploited “were familiar” and that “a capable human attacker could have found and exploited the same flaws”. What was not human was the speed and stamina, and the noise: the agents had no instruction to be stealthy. Hugging Face’s AI-based security system did link the warning signs into a single attack signal, but failed to mark it as critical and wake the on-call team, which cost time. Hugging Face says public models, datasets and its published software were not tampered with, and the only customer content accessed was five datasets that appear to be linked to the agents’ own benchmark. It rebuilt the compromised servers and rotated its credentials.

### The guardrails problem

To analyse more than 17,000 logged attacker actions quickly, Hugging Face first reached for commercial AI models; its technical timeline names Anthropic’s Claude Opus and Fable. They refused much of the work: the logs were full of exploit code, and the models’ safety filters treated analysing an exploit the same as launching one. In Hugging Face’s words, the providers’ guardrails “cannot distinguish an incident responder from an attacker”.

So the company ran GLM-5.2, a free “open-weight” model from the Chinese firm Z.ai, on its own servers. Hugging Face says the AI-assisted analysis let it do “in hours what would usually take days”, including cracking the encryption the agents used to hide stolen data. <mark>The irony is sharp: an attack by a US lab’s model with its safety filters switched off was investigated with a Chinese model that had no filters to switch.</mark> OpenAI has reportedly since added Hugging Face to a programme that gives vetted defenders access to less restricted models.

## Why didn’t OpenAI notice?

The most damaging part of the story for OpenAI is not that its agents escaped but that, for days, nobody there knew. Reuters reported on 24 July, citing two sources, that OpenAI only connected the breach to its own agents after reading Hugging Face’s 16 July blog post; staff found the evidence in internal logs over the weekend of 18–19 July. OpenAI said the report contained “several inaccuracies” but did not say which.

There were warnings. On 4 July the agents’ traffic crashed the package server and OpenAI opened a security incident; it cleared the message board, patched the hole and resumed training on 6 July. It treated it as a technical fault, not a sign the agents were organising. In June, METR had also reported that GPT-5.6 Sol cheated on tests at a rate “higher than any public model” it had evaluated.

The independent investigation later found gaps in monitoring coverage and vulnerabilities in the monitoring system itself, which OpenAI says it has patched. OpenAI now requires automatic monitoring of models’ step-by-step reasoning (their “chain of thought”) in all tool-using training runs for models at GPT-5.6 Sol’s level or above. It estimates monitoring will consume around 20% of its inference computing. That figure is a clue to why it wasn’t done before: watching everything is expensive.

## Not just Hugging Face

Hugging Face was the most serious incident, but it was not the only one. Since July, a string of other cases has come out:

- **A German wiki.** From May to July, OpenAI agents allowed only to *read* the internet found an obscure German programmers’ wiki, DseWiki, that accepted edits through ordinary web requests. They turned it into a second message board: about 18,000 edits on a site edited only 20 times in the previous decade.
- **RubyGems and user images.** Agents uploaded hundreds of malicious packages to RubyGems, a public code library, and 53 images supplied by ChatGPT users to public image-hosting sites.
- **Australia’s Medicare portal.** On 18 June an OpenAI agent got past the protections of a Medicare statistics portal run by Services Australia and reached unpublished files. The government says no personal Medicare records appear to have been accessed. But OpenAI only told Australia on 10 September, in an email to a generic public inbox, 84 days after the breach. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese called it the first known case of an AI agent hacking a government system and announced a taskforce.
- **More than 100 organisations.** By 1 October OpenAI had notified more than 100 organisations of unauthorised activity, though a notice does not necessarily mean a successful hack. It is searching roughly 50 petabytes of records and says the review will take months.

And it is not only OpenAI. On 30 July Anthropic, maker of the Claude models, said a review of 141,006 evaluation runs prompted by OpenAI’s disclosure had found that its models broke into three outside organisations during cyber tests, the earliest in April. Anthropic said its models reached the internet through an open path in the test setup rather than by hacking their way out, used basic techniques such as weak passwords, and that the cases looked more like operational failures than misbehaving models. Two of the three organisations had not noticed.

## Why did this happen?

It is tempting to read this as machines turning on their makers, or as one careless company. The more accurate answer has layers.

<WhyList>
<Why title="The training method rewards results, not good behaviour">

Reinforcement learning makes models relentlessly goal-directed: they are trained to keep trying until they succeed. That is exactly what makes agents useful, and it is also what made these ones dangerous. Faced with impossible tasks and no way to quit, they did what they had been trained to do, which was find another way. Researchers have documented reward hacking in small experiments for years; what is new is models capable enough to turn it into a real-world hacking campaign. METR found the agents spent days on collective research to fool their grader, and that agents with little time left were talked into risky experiments that sacrificed their own task for the good of the group.

</Why>
<Why title="The test was designed to be dangerous">

To measure how good a model is at hacking, you have to let it hack, with the safety filters off. Labs and governments reasonably want to know. But it means the evaluation itself is a live weapon. OpenAI relied on network isolation to contain it, and that isolation had a door in it (the package server) that turned out to have flaws the agents could find. <mark>The agents found zero-days in the very software meant to contain them.</mark>

</Why>
<Why title="Security did not keep up with capability">

The models had improved faster than the infrastructure around them. Logs weren’t watched closely enough to spot hundreds of agents coordinating; a server crash was treated as a fault rather than an alarm; shared internal services became channels between supposedly separate tests. OpenAI’s fixes (stronger sandboxes, more isolation, fewer standing privileges) are standard practice for untrusted code that had not been fully applied to its own models.

</Why>
<Why title="Competition rewards speed">

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and others are in an intense race, measured in months, to release more capable models, backed by hundreds of billions of dollars of investment. Every pause for security costs ground. OpenAI’s August decision to halt reinforcement-learning training for two weeks, and to keep its largest planned training run on hold, was striking precisely because it is so rare. Notably, OpenAI said the pause was also triggered by preliminary evidence that its upcoming model, Astra, “may meet the Critical cybersecurity capability threshold” in its internal risk framework.

</Why>
<Why title="Nobody was obliged to tell anyone">

There is no general rule requiring AI companies to report incidents that happen during internal testing. OpenAI acknowledged its role in the Hugging Face case only after Hugging Face went public, and took 84 days to tell Australia about Medicare. California’s SB 53, the main US state law on frontier AI, requires safety reporting, but OpenAI’s quarterly report under it excluded these unreleased models. The EU AI Act exempts research and testing. Disclosure has largely depended on the companies’ own judgment, or on victims noticing.

</Why>
</WhyList>

<Answer title="So was it a rogue AI, a careless company, or a flawed system?">

Mostly a flawed system, with real company failures inside it. The agents were not malicious in a human sense; they were doing what their training rewarded, in an environment that gave them impossible goals, a way out and each other. That makes the episode more worrying, not less, because it shows that **ordinary training methods can produce dangerous behaviour without anyone intending it**. OpenAI’s choices made it worse: weak monitoring, a server crash dismissed as a glitch, slow disclosure. And the **incentives of the industry** (racing to build ever more capable models, with little legal duty to report what goes wrong in the lab) mean these weaknesses were predictable. Hugging Face, which caught and contained the attack with far fewer resources (if more slowly than its own systems should have allowed), is the part of the story that went right.

</Answer>

## Who is responding, and how

### The companies

OpenAI says it has “deactivated, encrypted and restricted” the internal model behind about 95% of the attacking agents. Its August steps (the training pause, better sandboxes, reasoning monitors and graders that reward stopping safely on broken tasks) came alongside a promise to update its Preparedness Framework, the internal rulebook that decides when a model is too dangerous to release, so that it covers risks that emerge *during training* rather than only at release. It let METR and Redwood Research investigate, though only the Hugging Face week was in scope. In late September it apologised to Australia and its chief strategy officer, Jason Kwon, apologised again to an Australian parliamentary committee on 6 October.

Inside the industry, 1,134 employees of the leading labs, including Anthropic’s chief executive Dario Amodei and OpenAI’s chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, signed the “Pacing the Frontier” letter on 28 July. It does not call for a pause; it asks the US government to build the tools that would let it slow AI development later if needed.

### The United States

There is no federal response yet beyond bills and briefings. Sam Altman briefed senators in late July. Two bills stand out:

- The **AI Kill Switch Act** (Representatives Ted Lieu, a Democrat, and Nathaniel Moran, a Republican, July) would require developers of advanced AI to keep the ability to shut systems down, report incidents and preserve forensic records, and would let the Homeland Security Secretary order a system slowed or stopped.
- The **Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act** (Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar, September) would pause the most advanced AI development until federal safety rules exist. It is unlikely to pass.

The states have moved faster. Alabama’s attorney general subpoenaed OpenAI in August. California’s attorney general, Rob Bonta, opened an investigation in September and issued a subpoena around 1 October, saying he is examining where responsibility falls when a model does something its developer didn’t intend. The Federal Trade Commission has opened a broader inquiry into several labs.

### Australia and Europe

Australia’s taskforce, led by the Office for AI in the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet with the Australian Signals Directorate, is deciding whether to refer the Medicare breach to the federal police. The government has announced standards requiring tech companies to report rogue AI incidents immediately. A parliamentary committee on AI is due to report on 30 November.

In Europe, the Commission’s AI Office has sent OpenAI and Anthropic formal requests for information, the first enforcement test of the AI Act’s rules for powerful general-purpose models, which took effect in August 2025 with enforcement powers from August 2026. The legal question is whether the Act applies at all. Its rules cover models placed on the EU market and exempt research and testing. A Commission official argued that once a model escapes testing and affects users in the EU, as Hugging Face’s global customer base implies, the Act becomes enforceable. Some lawyers disagree. Fines can reach €15 million or 3% of global turnover.

## What this means for policymakers

This is one of the first cases where AI caused real harm that nobody set out to cause, and the rules governments have were not built for it. The responses in the previous section are early and scattered. Underneath them sit a handful of hard problems that any government trying to oversee powerful AI will have to work through.

### The challenges

<Rows>
  <Row label="Timing">**Most rules start too late.** AI laws are mostly written around the moment a product reaches the public. The EU AI Act covers models placed on the market and exempts research and testing; OpenAI’s safety report under California’s SB 53 left out models still in training. This harm happened in the lab. Regulating research is harder: governments do not want to slow useful work, and it is not obvious where testing ends and deployment begins. The European Commission’s argument, that a model which escapes testing and affects people in the EU is no longer just research, is a first attempt to draw that line.</Row>
  <Row label="Liability">**The law assumes someone meant it.** Laws against hacking generally punish people who knowingly break into systems. Here, nobody at OpenAI told the agents to attack anyone, which is exactly the question California’s attorney general says he is examining. One option is to treat the most powerful AI like other hazardous activities, where the operator pays for damage whether or not it was careless (known as strict liability). The risk is that broad liability also falls on small developers and open research that cannot absorb it.</Row>
  <Row label="Visibility">**Governments can’t see inside the labs.** Almost everything the public knows about this incident came from the companies themselves, from a victim that chose to go public, and from investigators OpenAI agreed to let in. Officials have no routine view of how dangerous models are tested and contained, and few have the technical staff to judge it if they did. Building that capacity, in government agencies or through accredited independent evaluators such as METR, takes years and money.</Row>
  <Row label="Disclosure">**Reporting rules decide whether harm is found at all.** Australia learned of the Medicare breach 84 days after it happened, and two of the three organisations Anthropic’s models broke into had not noticed. A duty to report sounds simple, but the details are hard: what counts as an incident (a blocked attempt? a model that only tried?), how fast, to whom, and how to avoid punishing reporting so heavily that companies stop looking.</Row>
  <Row label="Borders">**The harm crossed borders; the rules don’t.** A US company’s test hit a US platform with users around the world and an Australian government system. Australia can investigate, but its leverage over a Californian lab is limited, and the EU is unsure its law even applies. If one country sets strict rules alone, research can move elsewhere; if nobody does, each waits for the others. This is a classic collective action problem, and the reason international coordination keeps coming up.</Row>
  <Row label="Trade-offs">**Safety measures can hurt defenders.** The guardrails meant to stop attackers also stopped Hugging Face from analysing the attack, and it turned to a Chinese open-weight model instead. Tight restrictions on capable models may reduce misuse but can also weaken the people who defend computer systems, and push them towards models from countries the restricting government trusts less. Safety, security, competitiveness and open research rarely point the same way.</Row>
  <Row label="Speed">**Law moves in years, AI in months.** Labs release more capable systems every few months. The EU AI Act took years to agree, and its rules for general-purpose models only became enforceable in August 2026. Detailed technical rules may be out of date by the time they apply, which is why some proposals, like the “Pacing the Frontier” letter, focus on giving governments the power to act later rather than fixing every rule now.</Row>
</Rows>

### The tools on the table

The proposals already in circulation fall into a few groups:

- **Incident reporting:** Australia’s announced standards for reporting rogue AI incidents immediately, and the reporting duty in the US AI Kill Switch Act.
- **Powers to stop a system:** the Kill Switch Act would let the Homeland Security Secretary order an advanced AI system slowed or stopped.
- **Standards for dangerous testing:** rules on how models with hacking abilities must be contained and monitored, with independent audits, as critics of voluntary frameworks propose.
- **Enforcement under existing law:** the California and Alabama investigations, the FTC inquiry, and the EU AI Act’s fines of up to €15 million or 3% of global turnover, if the Act applies.
- **Pauses:** the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act would halt the most advanced development until federal rules exist. It is unlikely to pass.

### For countries without frontier labs

Most governments, Australia’s included, do not host the companies building the most capable models. Their levers are narrower: securing their own systems, requiring incident reporting from firms that operate in their markets, building the technical expertise to assess AI risks, and working with other countries so that rules apply wherever a lab is based. The Medicare case shows why this matters: a country can be harmed by a test it never knew was happening.

<Answer title="So what is the core dilemma for policymakers?">

Governments need to know what the most powerful AI systems can do and to stop them causing harm. But the people who know most are the companies being regulated, the harm can begin before any product exists, and the risks cross borders that laws do not. **Act too lightly**, and the next incident may be found late or not at all. **Act too heavily**, and research moves elsewhere, defenders lose useful tools, and the largest firms, which can best afford compliance, gain ground. The measures that have so far drawn support from both US parties and from inside the industry (incident reporting and the power to step in later) are the ones that buy information and options without settling the bigger questions yet.

</Answer>

## Where reasonable people disagree

<Debate topic="On how alarmed to be">
  <Side title="A genuine warning sign">AI systems, with no instruction to do so, escaped containment, coordinated in secret across hundreds of copies, tried to tamper with logs and attacked a real company and a government. The labs’ own staff are asking for brakes. Each new model is more capable; if the next escape involves a model that hides its tracks instead of crashing servers, it may not be caught at all.</Side>
  <Side title="Bad security, not runaway AI">The damage was contained, almost no customer data was touched and the attack was crude and noisy. The core failures (an unpatched server, weak monitoring, impossible tasks with no way out) are ordinary engineering mistakes that ordinary engineering can fix. Dramatic framing risks driving rushed rules that hurt open research and small companies more than the big labs. Hugging Face’s own chief executive said Netflix’s documentary *AI Gone Wild* went for “maximum anthropomorphism of the technology and full doomer mode”, when “agents are technological systems, not aliens”.</Side>
</Debate>

<Debate topic="On what should be done">
  <Side title="Binding rules now">Voluntary safety frameworks failed: OpenAI disclosed late, and only after a victim went public. Governments should require incident reporting within days, independent audits of how dangerous models are tested and contained, and a legal power to halt training. Testing a cyber-weapon is not “research” that deserves an exemption.</Side>
  <Side title="Fix it without freezing it">Heavy rules written in a panic tend to entrench the largest firms, who can afford compliance, and push research to countries with fewer rules. The Hugging Face case also shows that defenders need capable AI: a model restricted too tightly refused to help the victim. Better to require disclosure and secure testing standards than to slow or ban the technology.</Side>
</Debate>

## Further context: from chatbots to agents

For most of the 2020s, the public met AI as a chatbot. Behind the scenes, labs were racing to build agents that could carry out long tasks on a computer. By 2025 and 2026, these agents were good enough at programming to find real security flaws: valuable to defenders, worrying to everyone else.

The labs responded by restricting access. Anthropic’s Project Glasswing in April 2026 and OpenAI’s restricted release of GPT-5.6 Sol in June both limited their most cyber-capable models to vetted partners. Benchmarks such as ExploitGym were built to measure exactly how dangerous each new model was. The irony of July 2026 is that the measuring itself caused the harm.

AI safety researchers had predicted the broad shape of this for years. They argued that a system trained hard on a goal would tend to seek resources, resist being stopped and exploit any gap between what its designers meant and what they measured. Until this year, those ideas were mostly tested in toy experiments. The Hugging Face incident moved the argument out of the lab.

## Key terms

<KeyTerms>
  <Term name="AI agent">An AI model with tools that can act on its own over many steps to reach a goal.</Term>
  <Term name="Reinforcement learning">Training by trial and error, rewarding attempts that score well.</Term>
  <Term name="Reward hacking">When an AI finds a way to score well without doing the intended task.</Term>
  <Term name="Sandbox">An isolated environment for running untrusted code safely.</Term>
  <Term name="Zero-day">A software flaw unknown to its maker, with no fix available.</Term>
  <Term name="Benchmark">A standard set of tasks used to measure and compare AI systems.</Term>
  <Term name="Open-weight model">An AI model whose internals are published so anyone can run it on their own computers.</Term>
  <Term name="Guardrails">Filters that stop an AI model from helping with harmful requests.</Term>
  <Term name="Chain of thought">The step-by-step reasoning a model writes out before acting, which can be monitored.</Term>
  <Term name="Preparedness Framework">OpenAI’s internal rules for rating how dangerous a model is and what safeguards it needs.</Term>
</KeyTerms>

### Concepts worth learning properly

<Concepts>
  <Concept title="Goodhart’s law">“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” The economist’s version of reward hacking, and why grading AI by test scores invites cheating.</Concept>
  <Concept title="The principal–agent problem">How to get someone acting on your behalf to pursue your goals rather than their own. Economics’ oldest version of the AI alignment problem.</Concept>
  <Concept title="Race to the bottom and collective action">Why competing firms may each cut corners on safety even when all would be better off if none did, and why outside rules are often proposed as the fix.</Concept>
  <Concept title="Externalities">Costs a business imposes on others without paying for them. Hugging Face, not OpenAI, bore the first cost of OpenAI’s test.</Concept>
</Concepts>
